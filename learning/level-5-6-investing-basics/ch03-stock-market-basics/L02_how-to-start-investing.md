## Quick Spark
To start investing, you open a Demat account, complete your KYC and add money. You don't need to be rich to begin — you can start with as little as ₹500. Learn key terms like market orders, limit orders, dividends and the P/E ratio. And doing your own research before you buy beats guessing or blindly following trends!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: Getting Started with Stocks (Demat, KYC & How Much You Need)
Getting into the stock market takes a few simple steps. First, you open an account with a SEBI-registered stockbroker (such as Zerodha, Groww or Angel One), which links a Demat account (where your shares are stored safely in electronic form, like a digital locker), a trading account (which places your buy and sell orders) and your bank account. Second, you complete KYC (Know Your Customer) identity checks using your PAN card and Aadhaar, which keeps the system safe and prevents fraud. Third, you transfer money from your bank into your trading account. Then you can start investing with small amounts — even ₹500 can be enough to buy your first shares.
Example: When Maanu turns 18, she completes her online KYC with her PAN and Aadhaar in 10 minutes, transfers ₹1,000 from her savings account, and buys 2 shares of an Indian consumer company.

LEARNING POINT 2: Placing Orders: Market Orders vs. Limit Orders
When you buy or sell shares through your broker's app, you choose an order type. A market order buys or sells straight away at the best price available right now, so it is completed instantly. A limit order buys or sells only if the price reaches the level you set (or better). That gives you control over the price, but the trade may not happen if the market never reaches your limit.
Example: Rudy wants a share that is trading at ₹105. A market order buys it immediately at about ₹105, while a limit order set at ₹100 waits until the price falls to ₹100 before buying.

LEARNING POINT 3: Key Terms: Portfolio, Dividends & the P/E Ratio
Your portfolio is the full collection of all the shares, bonds and mutual funds you own. When a profitable company has more cash than it needs to grow, it may share part of its profit with shareholders as a cash dividend, paid into their bank accounts. To judge whether a share price is cheap or expensive compared with what the company earns, investors use the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio, which is the share price divided by the earnings per share. A P/E that is low compared with similar companies can be a sign the share may be undervalued.
Example: CA Jay looks at a company whose share costs ₹200 and which earns ₹20 profit per share, giving it a P/E ratio of 10. He also notes that it pays a ₹5 dividend per share every year.

## Real-Life Fact
India began replacing paper share certificates with electronic records in 1996, when NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited) was set up; CDSL (Central Depository Services Limited) followed in 1999. Today these two depositories hold investors' shares electronically, so lost or damaged certificates are a thing of the past.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Thinking you must buy 100 shares at a time or need lakhs of rupees before a broker will let you start.
Correction: In India you can buy just one share of any listed company, so beginners can start with only a few hundred rupees.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Open your account properly, start small with amounts you can afford, and always understand what you are buying.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What is the main job of a "Demat account"?
A. To store your clothes and books
B. To hold your shares and other securities safely in electronic form
C. To play online games
D. To print money at home
Hint: Demat stands for "dematerialised" (electronic) storage.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A Demat (dematerialised) account holds shares and securities in safe, electronic form with the central depositories NSDL and CDSL.

Q2. Which required process checks your identity using PAN and Aadhaar before you can trade shares in India?
A. KYC (Know Your Customer)
B. ATM PIN reset
C. A school admission test
D. Passport stamping
Hint: It is the standard identity check required by regulators.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: KYC (Know Your Customer) is the identity check SEBI requires to confirm who an investor is and to stop illegal activity.

Q3. How is a "limit order" different from a "market order"?
A. A limit order buys instantly at any price
B. A limit order goes through only at your chosen price or better, while a market order buys straight away at the current price
C. A limit order is only for buying gold
D. A limit order can only be placed on bank holidays
Hint: Limit orders let you control the exact price you pay.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A market order puts speed first and trades at the current price, while a limit order puts price first and trades only at your chosen price or better.

Q4. What is a "dividend" paid by a listed company?
A. A penalty charged to shareholders
B. A share of the company's profits paid to its shareholders
C. Interest on a personal loan
D. A tax collected by the city council
Hint: It is a cash reward shared with the company's owners out of its profits.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A dividend is a cash payment from the company's profits to its shareholders, paid per share.

Q5. If a company's share price is ₹300 and its yearly Earnings Per Share (EPS) is ₹30, what is its P/E ratio?
A. 10
B. 330
C. 9,000
D. 1
Hint: Divide the share price by the earnings per share (300 ÷ 30).
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio is Share Price ÷ EPS = 300 ÷ 30 = 10.

## Explore Further
Q1. What are NSDL and CDSL?
A. One is a private bank and the other is a supermarket chain
B. The two SEBI-registered central depositories that keep the electronic records of all Demat holdings
C. Political parties that run national elections
D. Apps for booking train tickets
Hint: They are the central digital vaults of India's markets.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: NSDL (promoted with the NSE) and CDSL (promoted by the BSE) are India's two SEBI-registered central depositories, which hold shares in electronic form.

Q2. How is a trailing 12-month (TTM) P/E ratio different from a forward P/E ratio?
A. TTM uses the company's actual earnings for the past 12 months, while forward P/E uses estimated future earnings
B. TTM only measures dividend yield
C. Forward P/E is only used for gold
D. TTM ignores the company's sales completely
Hint: TTM looks back at reported numbers; forward P/E looks ahead at forecasts.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Trailing P/E compares today's price with the last 12 months of reported earnings, while forward P/E uses analysts' forecasts for the coming period.

Q3. What is the "dividend yield" of a share?
A. The weight of the share certificate
B. The yearly dividend per share divided by the share price, shown as a percentage
C. The interest a broker charges for placing an order
D. How fast trades are completed
Hint: Calculate (Yearly Dividend per Share ÷ Share Price) × 100.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Dividend yield shows the yearly cash return from dividends alone, compared with the share's current price.

Q4. What is a "stop-loss order", and why do careful investors use it?
A. An order that automatically buys more shares when prices shoot up
B. An order to sell automatically if the price falls to a level you set, to limit your loss
C. An order that closes your bank account
D. A government tax order on company profits
Hint: It works like an automatic safety exit if a share price falls sharply.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: A stop-loss order automatically places a sell order when a share falls to a chosen price, helping to prevent very large losses.

Q5. Why should investors look beyond just a low P/E ratio when researching a company?
A. Calculating P/E ratios is illegal under Indian tax law
B. A very low P/E can be a "value trap" — a struggling business whose profits are falling, rather than a real bargain
C. P/E ratios only apply to property
D. Companies with a low P/E are not allowed to pay dividends
Hint: A cheap-looking share price can hide serious business problems.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: A low P/E can signal a business whose future profits are shrinking (a value trap), so investors also need to check its debt, management and cash flow.
