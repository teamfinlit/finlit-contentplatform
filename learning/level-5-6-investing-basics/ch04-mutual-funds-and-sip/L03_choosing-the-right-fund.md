# Choosing the Right Fund
**Level:** 5-6 Investing Basics | **Chapter:** 4 - Mutual Funds & SIP | **Topic:** 3

- Matching Funds to Goals
- Expense Ratio

## Quick Spark
Pick a mutual fund by matching the type of fund to your goal and to when you will need the money. For short goals (under 3 years), use safer debt funds; for long goals (5+ years), use equity and index funds. The expense ratio matters: over 10 years, about three out of four active large-cap funds in India have done worse than their index. Review your investments once a year and use research tools to stay on track!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: How to Pick a Mutual Fund & Check Its Track Record
Choosing a fund means looking at clear facts, not chasing yesterday's headlines. Start with the fund's long-term record over 5 and 10 years, through good and bad markets, rather than just last month's returns. For actively managed funds, check the fund manager's experience, whether the strategy has stayed consistent, and how often the fund buys and sells (its portfolio turnover). Research platforms and investment apps (such as Groww, Coin by Zerodha or Kuvera) let you compare a fund's performance with its benchmark index side by side.
Example: CA Jay helps Dia compare two funds by looking at how each did in the difficult years of the past decade, so she picks one with a proven record in both good and bad times.

LEARNING POINT 2: The Expense Ratio (Lower Is Better)
Every mutual fund charges a yearly fee for running the fund, called the expense ratio. It is a percentage of the fund's assets and is taken out of the NAV a little each day. Active equity funds typically charge around 0.8% to 1.8% a year, while passive index funds often charge less than 0.2%. A 1% difference sounds tiny, but over 20 to 30 years it compounds and can take away roughly 15% to 25% of your final wealth. Research also shows that most active funds fail to beat their index over long periods: over 10 years, about three out of four active large-cap funds in India have done worse than their benchmark.
Example: Maanu compares an active fund charging 1.5% a year with a NIFTY 50 index fund charging only 0.15%. Choosing the low-cost index fund could save her lakhs of rupees in fees over her working life.

LEARNING POINT 3: Matching Funds to Goals and Time Horizon
The most important step is matching the type of fund to when you will need the money. For short-term goals (under 3 years, like a holiday or school fees), protect your money with debt funds or liquid funds, which are not exposed to the share market's swings. For medium-term goals (3 to 5 years, like buying a vehicle), balanced advantage or hybrid funds offer moderate growth with some protection when markets fall. For long-term goals (5+ years, like higher education or buying a home), use diversified equity and index funds. For retirement (20+ years away), a larger share in equity, including some mid-cap and small-cap funds, gives compounding the most room to work.
Example: Rudy's family saves for a home repair in 2 years using a safe liquid fund, while they invest for Rudy's university degree, 12 years away, in an equity index fund.

LEARNING POINT 4: Direct vs. Regular Plans & a Yearly Review
Every mutual fund comes in two versions: direct plans (bought straight from the fund house, with no distributor commission) and regular plans (which include an ongoing commission paid to a distributor or broker). Direct plans always have a lower expense ratio, so their NAV grows faster and long-term returns are higher. Finally, make a habit of reviewing your investments once a year to rebalance and replace funds that keep doing badly.
Example: Dia switches her mutual funds from regular plans to direct plans on an investment app, cutting out the middleman's commission and raising her yearly returns.

## Real-Life Fact
S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA India report for 2025 found that 76% of actively managed Indian large-cap funds did worse than their benchmark index over 10 years, and 84% did worse over 5 years — a big reason low-cost index funds are so popular.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Choosing a mutual fund only because it gave the highest return in the last 6 months.
Correction: Short-term top performers change often and may be taking big risks. Choose funds based on low expense ratios, a sensible mix of investments and how long you plan to stay invested.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Match your fund to your time horizon, keep expense ratios low, choose direct plans, and review your plan once a year.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What is the expense ratio of a mutual fund?
A. The fee the government charges to print banknotes
B. The yearly percentage fee taken from the fund's assets to pay for running and managing it
C. A fine for selling units after 10 years
D. The cost of visiting a bank branch
Hint: It is the ongoing management fee charged by the fund house.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: The expense ratio is the yearly percentage of a fund's assets used to pay for managing the portfolio, administration and marketing.

Q2. If you need your money for a goal in less than 2 years, which type of fund suits you best?
A. Small-cap equity funds
B. High-risk tech sector funds
C. Liquid funds or short-term debt funds
D. Speculative derivative funds
Hint: Short time frames need safety and small price swings.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: Money needed within about 3 years is best kept in debt or liquid funds, so a share-market fall can't eat into it just when you need it.

Q3. Why do low-cost index funds (expense ratio under 0.2%) often do better than actively managed funds over long periods?
A. Index funds are exempt from maths
B. Low fees leave more money to compound, and most active managers fail to beat the market index consistently
C. Index funds promise 50% guaranteed returns
D. Active managers are not allowed to buy good shares
Hint: Lower yearly fees mean more of your money keeps compounding.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Low fees and simply tracking the market avoid both fee drag and stock-picking mistakes, so index funds beat most active funds over time.

Q4. What is the key difference between a "direct plan" and a "regular plan"?
A. Direct plans are only for commercial banks
B. Direct plans pay no distributor commission, so they have a lower expense ratio and higher returns than regular plans
C. Regular plans never invest in shares
D. Direct plans expire after 6 months
Hint: Direct plans skip the middleman and save the commission.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Direct plans have lower expense ratios because no commission is paid to brokers or distributors, which leads to a higher NAV and better long-term returns.

Q5. How often should a disciplined long-term investor do a proper review of their investments?
A. Every 10 minutes during the trading day
B. Once a year, to check progress towards goals and rebalance
C. Once every 50 years
D. Never, under any circumstances
Hint: A yearly check keeps you on track without causing emotional over-reactions.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A yearly review is enough to rebalance and track your goals without falling into the trap of over-trading.

## Explore Further
Q1. What is a mutual fund's "portfolio turnover ratio"?
A. How often the fund manager moves office
B. The percentage of the fund's holdings bought or sold over the past year, showing how often it trades
C. The interest rate on savings accounts
D. The number of investors who left the fund
Hint: It shows how actively the manager buys and sells shares.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: The portfolio turnover ratio measures how much of the fund is bought and sold over 12 months; high turnover usually means higher trading costs.

Q2. What does the "Sharpe ratio" measure when you study a fund's track record?
A. How sharp the paper statements are
B. The extra return a fund earns above a risk-free rate for each unit of risk (volatility) it takes
C. The number of complaints filed with SEBI
D. How many years the fund manager spent at school
Hint: Named after Nobel Prize winner William Sharpe, it measures return adjusted for risk.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: The Sharpe ratio = (Fund Return − Risk-Free Rate) ÷ Volatility of the fund's returns. It shows whether a fund's extra return comes from skill or just from taking more risk.

Q3. What is an "Equity Linked Savings Scheme" (ELSS)?
A. A debt fund with a 30-year lock-in
B. A tax-saving equity mutual fund that, under the old tax regime, qualifies for a deduction of up to ₹1.5 lakh (Section 80C of the old Income-tax Act, now Section 123), with a 3-year lock-in
C. A foreign currency trading account
D. A loan scheme for buying property
Hint: ELSS has the shortest lock-in (3 years) of the main tax-saving options.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: ELSS is an equity mutual fund that qualifies for the ₹1.5 lakh tax deduction under the old tax regime, with a 3-year lock-in — the shortest among these tax-saving investments. The deduction is not available under the new tax regime.

Q4. What is the main purpose of a "dynamic asset allocation" (or balanced advantage) fund?
A. Investing 100% in risky foreign tech start-ups
B. Automatically shifting money between equity and debt based on how expensive the market looks, to capture gains while limiting falls
C. Removing all taxes
D. Guaranteeing double-digit interest every week
Hint: It changes its share and bond mix depending on whether markets look cheap or expensive.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Balanced advantage funds adjust their equity–debt mix using valuation measures (like P/E or P/B ratios) to manage market risk.

Q5. What is an "exit load" in mutual funds?
A. A fee for downloading the investment app
B. A small fee the fund house deducts if you sell your units before a minimum holding period
C. A government tax on opening a bank account
D. A holiday bonus paid to unitholders
Hint: It discourages quick selling by charging a small percentage if you exit too soon.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: An exit load is a fee charged if you redeem units before a set minimum period (for example, 1% if you sell within 1 year).
