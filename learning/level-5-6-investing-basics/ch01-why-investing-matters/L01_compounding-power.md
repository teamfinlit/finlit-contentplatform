# Compounding Power
**Level:** 5-6 Investing Basics | **Chapter:** 1 - Why Investing Matters | **Topic:** 1

## Key Concepts
- Compound Interest = Interest on Interest
- ₹10,000/Month SIP at 12% for 20 Years
- Start Early
- Rule of 72

## Quick Spark
Compounding means earning returns not just on the money you put in, but also on all the returns you've already earned. Invest ₹10,000 every month at about 12% a year and in 20 years it can grow to roughly ₹1 crore — even though you only put in ₹24 lakh yourself. Time does most of the heavy lifting, which is why starting early is your biggest advantage. And with the Rule of 72, you can work out how fast your money doubles in seconds!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: Compound Interest — Earning Returns on Returns
With simple interest, you earn interest only on the money you started with. Compounding goes further: your earnings are added back in, so next time you earn on your original money plus all the earlier gains. It works like a snowball rolling down a hill — small at first, but it picks up more snow with every turn and keeps getting bigger, faster.
Example: Rudy puts ₹100 into a piggy bank that grows by 10% each year. Year 1 adds ₹10, making ₹110. Year 2 earns 10% on ₹110, adding ₹11 to reach ₹121. By Year 10 it has grown to about ₹259 — more than double — without Rudy adding a single extra rupee.

LEARNING POINT 2: ₹10,000/Month SIP at 12% for 20 Years — The ₹1 Crore Milestone
A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) lets you put a fixed amount into an investment fund every month. If you invest ₹10,000 a month for 20 years and earn an average of about 12% a year, you put in ₹24 lakh of your own money — but your investment can grow to about ₹1 crore. That means roughly three-quarters of the final amount comes from compounding growth, not from your own savings.
Example: Dia sets up an automatic SIP of ₹10,000 a month. Over 20 years she invests ₹24 lakh, and compounding grows her fund to about ₹1 crore.

LEARNING POINT 3: Start Early — The Cost of Waiting
Time is the most important ingredient in compounding — far more important than trying to pick the perfect day to invest. The biggest gains come in the final years, when your money pile is largest, so every extra year at the start adds a big chunk at the end. Waiting even a few years means you have to invest much more every month just to catch up.
Example: Maanu starts investing ₹5,000 a month at age 20. Her friend invests the same ₹5,000 a month but starts at 25. At 12% a year, by age 50 Maanu has about ₹1.76 crore while her friend has about ₹95 lakh — Maanu ends up with nearly double, just by starting five years earlier. To catch up, her friend would need to invest about ₹9,300 a month instead.

LEARNING POINT 4: The Rule of 72 — How Fast Does Money Double?
The Rule of 72 is a quick mental shortcut for how many years it takes your money to double at a fixed yearly return. Just divide 72 by the yearly return percentage: 72 ÷ Return Rate = Years to Double. At 12%, money doubles about every 6 years (72 ÷ 12 = 6). Over 24 years, that's four doublings — roughly 16 times your original amount.
Example: CA Jay shows Rudy that at 8% interest, savings double about every 9 years (72 ÷ 8 = 9), while at 12% they double in about 6 years.

## Real-Life Fact
The quote calling compound interest the "eighth wonder of the world" is often attributed to Albert Einstein, though there's no evidence he actually said it — but the idea behind it is sound: those who understand compounding earn it, and those who don't end up paying it.
Legendary investor Warren Buffett built the vast majority of his wealth after his 50th birthday. He started investing as a child, and decades of compounding made his later years the most powerful — a real-world example of how compounding gains pile up at the end.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Thinking you have to wait until you have a big lump sum (like ₹1,00,000) before you can start investing.
Correction: Compounding rewards time far more than starting size. Many mutual fund SIPs let you start with as little as ₹500 a month, and starting small but early can build more wealth than starting late with a large amount.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Start investing early and let compounding multiply your money over time.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What is the main difference between simple interest and compound interest?
A. Simple interest is only used for buying toys
B. Compound interest earns returns on both your starting money and all your past gains
C. Simple interest doubles your money every 6 months
D. Compound interest is only paid in gold coins
Hint: Compound growth builds on top of earlier gains.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Compound interest is calculated on the growing total (your starting money plus past returns), which is what creates faster and faster growth.

Q2. If you invest ₹10,000 every month through an SIP at about 12% a year for 20 years, roughly how much could your investment grow to?
A. ₹24 lakh
B. ₹50 lakh
C. ₹1 crore
D. ₹5 crore
Hint: You put in ₹24 lakh yourself, and compounding adds a lot more on top.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: A monthly SIP of ₹10,000 for 20 years at about 12% a year grows to roughly ₹1 crore, of which only ₹24 lakh is your own money.

Q3. Using the Rule of 72, about how many years does it take for an investment to double at 12% a year?
A. 12 years
B. 6 years
C. 7.2 years
D. 24 years
Hint: Divide 72 by 12.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: 72 ÷ 12 = 6, so money doubles about every 6 years at a 12% yearly return.

Q4. Why does starting to invest 5 years earlier make such a big difference to your final amount?
A. Early investors never pay tax
B. The extra years let compounding keep working, and the biggest gains come at the end when your money pile is largest
C. Stock exchanges give free bonus shares to younger people
D. Banks double all accounts opened before age 25
Hint: Compounding gains are biggest in the final years.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Compounding grows slowly at first and fastest at the end, so extra years at the start add the most money to the final total.

Q5. Using the Rule of 72, roughly what does ₹10,000 become after 24 years at 12% a year?
A. It doubles once to ₹20,000
B. It doubles 4 times (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16×) to reach about ₹1,60,000
C. It stays at ₹10,000
D. It grows to only ₹50,000
Hint: Doubling every 6 years means 24 years gives 4 doublings (₹10k → ₹20k → ₹40k → ₹80k → ₹160k).
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: At 12%, money doubles about every 6 years, so 24 years gives 4 doublings: ₹10,000 × 16 = about ₹1,60,000. (The Rule of 72 is an estimate — the exact figure is about ₹1,51,800.)

## Explore Further
Q1. Which formula gives the future value (FV) of a lump sum (P) compounded once a year at rate r for n years?
A. FV = P × (1 + r × n)
B. FV = P × (1 + r)ⁿ
C. FV = P ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ
D. FV = P + (r × n)
Hint: Compounding raises the growth factor to the power of the number of years.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: FV = P × (1 + r)ⁿ multiplies the money by (1 + r) once for every year, which is what makes the growth exponential. Option A is the simple-interest formula.

Q2. Why does the Rule of 72 work well for estimating doubling time at typical interest rates?
A. 72 is the number of months in a decade
B. The exact doubling number is close to 69.3 (from the natural logarithm of 2 ≈ 0.693), and 72 is a convenient nearby number that divides evenly by many rates such as 6, 8, 9 and 12
C. It was created by the World Bank in 1944
D. It only applies to physical gold
Hint: ln(2) ≈ 0.693, and 72 is easy to divide.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: The mathematically exact doubling time is about 69.3 ÷ rate for continuous compounding, but 72 divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12 and gives accurate estimates for yearly compounding at typical rates.

Q3. What happens to the Effective Annual Rate (EAR) when interest is compounded more often, such as monthly instead of yearly?
A. The EAR becomes slightly higher than the stated yearly rate
B. Returns drop to zero
C. It makes no difference at all
D. Returns are cut in half
Hint: Reinvesting gains sooner means earning interest on interest faster.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: More frequent compounding reinvests gains sooner, raising the effective rate: EAR = (1 + i/n)ⁿ − 1. For example, 12% compounded monthly gives an EAR of about 12.68%.

Q4. In retirement planning, what does the "cost of waiting" show?
A. The fee banks charge for opening an account late
B. How much more you must invest every month later in life to match someone who started early
C. The shipping cost of share certificates
D. The tax penalty for saving in cash
Hint: Delaying your start means bigger contributions later.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Starting late means you lose years of compounding, so you must invest much larger amounts each month to reach the same goal.

Q5. If inflation stays at 7% a year for 20 years, what happens to cash that is kept uninvested?
A. Its value doubles
B. It loses about three-quarters of its buying power, even though the amount in the account doesn't change
C. The central bank replaces it with new coins
D. The bank balance falls to zero
Hint: Inflation compounds in reverse, shrinking buying power year after year.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Inflation compounds too, but against you. At 7% a year for 20 years, ₹1 buys only about 26 paise worth of today's goods (1 ÷ (1.07)²⁰ ≈ 0.258), a loss of roughly 74% of buying power.
