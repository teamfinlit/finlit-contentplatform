# Sector Rotation Basics
**Level:** 9-10 Advanced Investing | **Chapter:** 2 - Advanced Instruments | **Topic:** 3

## Key Concepts
- Early recovery: financials, real estate
- Mid cycle: industrials, tech
- Late cycle: energy, materials
- Recession: consumer staples, healthcare, utilities
- Allocate 10–15% as a "satellite" bet

## Quick Spark
Different industry sectors tend to lead the stock market in different phases of the economic cycle. Early recovery has historically favoured banks and housing, mid-cycle has boosted tech, and recessions have favoured healthcare and consumer staples. Timing sector shifts perfectly is notoriously difficult for everyday investors. Keep broad index funds as your core foundation and limit sector bets to a 10–15% satellite allocation!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: The Economic Cycle & Sector Dynamics
The broad economy moves through four recurring phases: Early Recovery, Mid-Cycle Expansion, Late-Cycle Boom, and Recession. Because consumer demand, interest rates, and business investment change in each stage, some industry sectors see profits surge while others struggle. Understanding these patterns helps investors recognise which sectors have historically done well in each economic climate.
Example: Rudy notices that when everyone is excited and finding jobs, people buy new apartments and gadgets, but when times get tough, families spend only on basic groceries and medicine.

LEARNING POINT 2: Mapping Sectors to Economic Phases
Historical market data links sector leadership to the four economic phases:
1. Early Recovery: As central banks lower interest rates and activity rebounds, rate-sensitive and consumer discretionary sectors like Financials (Banks), Real Estate, and Automobiles tend to lead.
2. Mid-Cycle Expansion: As growth matures and businesses invest more, Industrials, Technology, and Capital Goods tend to benefit from strong corporate spending.
3. Late-Cycle Peak: As inflation heats up and capacity tightens, commodity-driven sectors like Energy (Oil & Gas) and Basic Materials (Metals & Mining) tend to outperform on rising resource prices.
4. Recession / Contraction: In slowdowns, defensive sectors like Consumer Staples (FMCG), Healthcare / Pharmaceuticals, and Utilities (Power & Water) tend to hold up best, because households must buy food and medicine whatever the economy is doing.
Example: During a market downturn, Dia's investments in pharmaceutical and FMCG companies stay relatively stable because people keep buying medicine and soap every day.

LEARNING POINT 3: The Sector Timing Trap
Sector rotation sounds neat on paper, but predicting the exact turning points of economic cycles in real time is notoriously difficult. Economic data looks backwards, cycles vary in length, and sector moves often happen months before official economic announcements. Investors who keep shuffling all their money between hot sectors often buy near the top and sell near the bottom, paying extra capital gains tax and trading costs along the way.
Example: Maanu jumps into metal stocks after a huge rally, only for global commodity prices to cool the following week, leaving her with sudden losses.

LEARNING POINT 4: The Core-Satellite Portfolio Framework
To balance stability with targeted opportunities, many investors use a Core-Satellite strategy. Your Core (85% to 90%) is made of broad-market index funds (like NIFTY 50 and NIFTY Next 50) that you hold for decades. Your Satellite (10% to 15%) can go into sector or thematic bets, so that even if a sector bet fails, your core family wealth stays safe.
Example: CA Jay helps Dia build a portfolio with 85% in broad index funds (Core) and 15% split between an IT sector fund and a healthcare fund (Satellite) to aim for extra upside safely.

## Real-Life Fact
A well-known study by Gary Brinson, Randolph Hood and Gilbert Beebower (1986) found that a portfolio's asset allocation policy — the mix of shares, bonds and cash — explained over 90% of the ups and downs in large pension funds' returns over time, while stock picking and market timing explained much less.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Moving your entire portfolio into whichever sector performed best over the past 6 months.
Correction: Sectors are cyclical and tend to revert to the mean; last year's top-performing sector is often next year's laggard. Never give up broad diversification for concentrated sector bets.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Anchor 85–90% of your wealth in broad-market index funds and limit tactical sector bets to a small 10–15% satellite slice.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. Which sectors have historically led the market during the "Early Recovery" phase of an economic cycle?
A. Gold mines only
B. Rate-sensitive sectors like Financials (Banks), Real Estate, and Autos
C. Utilities and government offices
D. Space exploration start-ups
Hint: Low interest rates and rebounding borrowing help banks and housing.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: In an early recovery, lower interest rates and rebounding demand for credit tend to lift financials, real estate, and consumer discretionary sectors.

Q2. Why are Consumer Staples (FMCG) and Healthcare called "defensive sectors"?
A. They manufacture military defence equipment
B. Demand for food, soap, and medicine stays stable whether the economy is booming or in recession
C. They are exempt from all stock market rules
D. They only operate during wars
Hint: People need basic food and healthcare even in a downturn.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Defensive sectors provide necessities (groceries, medicine, power) that people must buy in all economic conditions.

Q3. Which sectors typically outperform during the "Late-Cycle Peak", when inflation is high and production capacity is tight?
A. Energy, commodities, and basic materials (metals)
B. Luxury cruise lines
C. Early-stage, loss-making tech start-ups
D. Local bookstores
Hint: High inflation and strong demand for resources push up oil and metal prices.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Late in an expansion, factories run near full capacity and commodity prices rise, which benefits energy, mining, and basic materials companies.

Q4. In the Core-Satellite strategy, what share of your portfolio is recommended for sector or thematic bets?
A. 100% in a single sector
B. Around 10% to 15% (the Satellite)
C. Exactly 0%
D. 90% in speculative options
Hint: Keep sector bets to a small, contained side portion.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A core-satellite approach puts 85%–90% in broad index funds (the core) and caps sector bets at 10%–15% (the satellite) to contain risk.

Q5. Why is constantly rotating your whole portfolio between sectors dangerous for individual investors?
A. It makes stock charts turn black and white
B. Sector rotations happen quickly and unpredictably, so traders often buy near cyclical tops and pay heavy taxes and fees
C. Central banks ban investors from changing sectors
D. Sector funds do not pay dividends
Hint: Timing mistakes and trading costs eat into long-term compounding.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Predicting economic turning points is very hard; switching your whole portfolio between sectors leads to whipsaws, tax leakage, and underperformance.

## Explore Further
Q1. What is the difference between a "cyclical stock" and a "defensive" (counter-cyclical) stock?
A. Cyclical stocks make bicycles; defensive stocks make cars
B. A cyclical company's earnings rise and fall with the economy, while a defensive company's earnings stay relatively steady through the cycle
C. Cyclical stocks only trade in December
D. There is no economic difference
Hint: Cyclicals follow GDP up and down; defensives see steady demand in all phases.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Cyclical companies (steel, autos, construction) depend heavily on economic expansions, whereas defensive companies (staples, pharma) keep steadier earnings in recessions.

Q2. What has an inverted yield curve (short-term government bond yields higher than long-term yields) historically signalled?
A. A massive economic boom starting tomorrow
B. A possible economic slowdown or recession, often within about the next 12 to 18 months
C. Permanent zero inflation
D. The immediate shutdown of all stock exchanges
Hint: An inverted yield curve is one of the most watched warning signs of a recession.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: An inverted yield curve reflects tight monetary policy and expectations of slower growth, and in the past it has often come before recessions, though not every inversion has been followed by one.

Q3. What is the beta of a defensive utility stock typically expected to be, compared with the broader market?
A. Well above 2.5
B. Below 1.0 (meaning smaller price swings than the overall index)
C. Negative 100
D. Exactly zero at all times
Hint: Defensive utilities move less than the broader market.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Defensive stocks usually have a low beta (β < 1.0), meaning their prices swing less than the broader market.

Q4. What is "mean reversion" in sector performance over several years?
A. Sectors that outperform strongly in one cycle tend to see lower relative returns later, as money floods in and valuations normalise
B. Sector prices become mean and unfriendly
C. A government rule that resets all stock prices to ₹100
D. All companies merging into one state enterprise
Hint: Extreme outperformance or underperformance tends to drift back towards long-term averages.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Mean reversion suggests that returns and valuation multiples tend to move back towards their long-term averages over time.

Q5. What is a "thematic fund", and how does it differ from a sector fund?
A. Thematic funds only invest in government bonds
B. A thematic fund invests across several industries linked by one big trend (e.g. green energy or digitalisation), whereas a sector fund focuses on a single industry
C. Thematic funds never charge expense ratios
D. Sector funds are only managed by foreign banks
Hint: Themes span several related sectors tied to one big trend.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Thematic funds target trends that cut across industries (like the EV ecosystem or manufacturing revival), whereas sector funds stick to one industry (like banking or pharma).
