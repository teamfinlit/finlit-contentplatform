# ETFs & Index Funds Deep Dive
**Level:** 9-10 Advanced Investing | **Chapter:** 2 - Advanced Instruments | **Topic:** 2

- NIFTY/SENSEX
- Expense ratio
- Demat

## Quick Spark
ETFs trade continuously on stock exchanges like individual shares, whereas index funds are bought and sold once a day at NAV. Index funds allow automated monthly SIPs without needing a Demat account, making them ideal for long-term consistency. Putting 5–15% into international equity funds (like S&P 500 or NASDAQ 100 funds) reduces single-country risk. Remember to consider foreign withholding taxes and currency movements when investing globally!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: ETFs vs. Index Funds: Mechanical Differences
Both Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) and Index Mutual Funds passively copy market benchmarks (like the NIFTY 50, SENSEX, or NIFTY Next 50) with low tracking error. But they work differently: an ETF is listed on stock exchanges (NSE/BSE), so you need a Demat and trading account to buy and sell it during the day at changing live prices. An Index Mutual Fund is bought through the fund house, letting you buy units — including fractions of a unit — at the single end-of-day Net Asset Value (NAV), without needing a broker.
Example: Rudy buys 1 unit of a NIFTY 50 ETF at 11:30 AM through his broker app at the live price of ₹250; Dia's automated order for an index mutual fund, placed before the 3:00 PM cut-off, is processed at that day's closing NAV.

LEARNING POINT 2: Expense Ratios & SIP Suitability
ETFs often have slightly lower headline expense ratios (0.05% to 0.10%) than index mutual funds (0.10% to 0.25%). However, buying ETFs adds other costs such as brokerage, exchange charges, Demat maintenance fees, and bid-ask spreads. For disciplined, automated monthly investing, index mutual funds are usually better for SIPs because bank auto-debits buy units automatically, whereas ETF SIPs need manual orders or a special broker set-up.
Example: Maanu runs an automated ₹1,000 monthly SIP into an index mutual fund so her savings happen without her touching them, avoiding the hassle of placing trades every month.

LEARNING POINT 3: International Diversification: S&P 500 & NASDAQ 100
Investing only in your home market ties your whole net worth to one country's economy. Putting a sensible 5% to 15% of your long-term equity portfolio into developed global markets (such as US-focused S&P 500 or tech-heavy NASDAQ 100 index funds) adds valuable geographic diversification. Because different economies move through different business cycles, overseas holdings can help balance your returns when the domestic market slows.
Example: CA Jay helps Dia put 10% of her equity portfolio into an international US index fund, giving her family part-ownership of global tech giants like Apple, Microsoft, and Google.

LEARNING POINT 4: Currency Movements & Foreign Withholding Taxes
When investing internationally, two extra factors come into play. Currency movements: if the Indian rupee weakens against the US dollar, your foreign investment is worth more in rupee terms. Foreign withholding taxes: other countries often deduct tax on dividends at source — for example, 25% on US dividends for Indian residents under the India–US tax treaty. Factor both into your expected net returns.
Example: If Maanu's US index investment grows by 8% in dollars while the dollar strengthens 3% against the rupee, her total return in rupee terms is about 11% before taxes.

## Real-Life Fact
Passive funds — ETFs and index funds — managed about ₹14.1 lakh crore in India as of March 2026, up 23% in a year according to AMFI data, helped by large investments from the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) into NIFTY and SENSEX ETFs.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Assuming ETFs are always cheaper than index funds for small monthly SIPs, without counting brokerage charges and bid-ask spreads.
Correction: Although ETF expense ratios look slightly lower, trading fees, transaction costs and wide bid-ask spreads in less-traded ETFs often make index mutual funds cheaper and more convenient for monthly SIPs.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Use low-cost index funds for automated domestic SIPs and add a 5–15% international allocation for global risk protection.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What is the main structural difference between an ETF and an Index Mutual Fund?
A. ETFs are illegal in India
B. ETFs trade continuously on stock exchanges during market hours like shares, while index funds are bought and sold at the end-of-day NAV
C. Index funds only hold physical real estate
D. ETFs never track market indices
Hint: ETFs trade during the day on the NSE/BSE.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: ETFs trade throughout the trading session at live market prices on exchanges, whereas index mutual funds transact once a day at the closing NAV.

Q2. Why are index mutual funds generally preferred over ETFs for automated monthly SIPs?
A. Index funds make all future shopping free
B. They allow seamless bank auto-debits and fractional units without manual Demat orders or brokerage fees
C. ETFs are forbidden from holding technology stocks
D. Index funds guarantee double-digit profits every week
Hint: Index funds automate buying fractional units without manual trading.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Index mutual funds allow fully automated SIP debits and fractional units without brokerage fees or placing orders during market hours.

Q3. What is a commonly recommended range for international equity exposure to achieve geographic diversification?
A. 100% international only
B. Around 5% to 15%
C. Exactly 0%
D. 80% in single speculative foreign stocks
Hint: A modest slice reduces single-country risk without too much currency exposure.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Financial planners commonly suggest putting about 5%–15% of equity exposure into global indices to reduce country-specific risk.

Q4. How does the rupee weakening against the US dollar affect your international investments?
A. It wipes out all international returns completely
B. It boosts the value of your foreign investment when converted back into rupees
C. It freezes your bank account
D. It has no impact at all
Hint: When the foreign currency becomes more valuable, your foreign assets are worth more rupees.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: When the rupee weakens against the currency of a foreign asset, that asset is worth more in rupee terms.

Q5. What is a "foreign withholding tax" in international investing?
A. A tax on buying flight tickets
B. A tax deducted at source by a foreign country on dividends before they reach international investors
C. A fine paid for holding bank accounts
D. An insurance fee charged on international shipping cargo
Hint: It is taken out of foreign dividends in the country where they are paid.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Withholding tax is charged by a foreign country on income (like dividends) earned by non-resident investors, with rates set by Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs).

## Explore Further
Q1. What is the role of an "Authorized Participant" (AP) in creating and redeeming ETF units?
A. Printing marketing brochures for retail banks
B. Taking advantage of price gaps by creating or redeeming large blocks of ETF units with the fund house, which keeps the ETF's market price close to its NAV
C. Collecting income taxes on behalf of city councils
D. Setting government interest rates
Hint: Large market makers keep ETF prices close to the value of what the ETF holds.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Authorized Participants create and redeem large ETF "creation units" with the fund house, and this arbitrage keeps ETF market prices close to their underlying NAV.

Q2. What is "iNAV" (Indicative Net Asset Value), shown on stock exchange terminals during market hours?
A. An imaginary number generated by algorithms
B. A real-time estimate of an ETF's per-unit NAV, updated every few seconds from the prices of the stocks it holds
C. The ETF's price from 10 years ago
D. The total tax owed by the ETF manager
Hint: It shows the ETF's fair value live throughout the trading day.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: iNAV is a continuous intraday estimate of an ETF's fair value per unit, based on the live market prices of its underlying holdings.

Q3. What is the yearly limit under the Reserve Bank of India's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) for resident individuals sending money abroad?
A. USD 25,000
B. USD 250,000 per financial year
C. Unlimited foreign currency
D. USD 1,000
Hint: RBI allows residents to send up to a quarter of a million US dollars a year.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Under RBI's LRS, resident individuals can send up to USD 250,000 per financial year abroad for permitted purposes, including investing in overseas shares.

Q4. What Tax Collected at Source (TCS) rate applies to LRS remittances for investment purposes above ₹10 lakh in a financial year?
A. 0%
B. 20% (which can be adjusted against your overall tax liability)
C. 50%
D. 1%
Hint: TCS on investment remittances above the yearly threshold is 20%.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: LRS remittances for investment above ₹10 lakh in a financial year attract 20% TCS on the amount above the threshold, which can be claimed as a credit against income tax payable. (The threshold was raised from ₹7 lakh to ₹10 lakh from April 2025.)

Q5. What is a "Fund of Funds" (FoF) structure used for international investing in India?
A. A fund that gives away free money to charities
B. A domestic mutual fund that pools rupees and invests them in an overseas ETF or international index fund
C. A government loan given to start-up companies
D. An unregulated cryptocurrency wallet
Hint: A domestic fund that holds units of another overseas fund.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: An international Fund of Funds (FoF) is a domestic mutual fund that invests in an overseas fund or ETF, letting investors access global markets in rupees.
