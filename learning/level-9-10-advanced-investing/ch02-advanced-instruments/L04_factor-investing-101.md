# Factor Investing 101
**Level:** 9-10 Advanced Investing | **Chapter:** 2 - Advanced Instruments | **Topic:** 4

## Key Concepts
- Value
- Momentum
- Quality
- Size
- Low volatility
- Indian factor indices

## Quick Spark
Factor investing targets specific, well-researched company characteristics that have driven long-term returns. The five classic factors are Value, Momentum, Quality, Size, and Low Volatility. Factor indices (like NIFTY 200 Momentum 30, NIFTY 100 Quality 30 and NIFTY 100 Low Volatility 30) apply these rules automatically. Combining complementary factors gives you rule-based, objective investing without manager bias!

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: What Is Factor Investing (Smart Beta)?
Traditional index funds weight companies purely by their market capitalisation (total company value). Factor investing — often called Smart Beta or rule-based quantitative investing — goes a step further by choosing and weighting stocks using specific, measurable characteristics ("factors") that have historically driven returns. Built on decades of academic research, factor strategies systematically target company traits that have delivered better risk-adjusted returns across market cycles.
Example: Rudy and Maanu pick a school football squad; instead of simply choosing the tallest players (like market-cap weighting), they select players by measured running speed, passing accuracy, and stamina (like factor weighting).

LEARNING POINT 2: The 5 Classic Investment Factors
Academic finance highlights five foundational factors that help explain stock market returns:
1. Value: Investing in sound companies trading at low valuations (low P/E, low price-to-book, or high dividend yield). Bargain stocks have tended to outperform over long periods as their valuations normalise.
2. Momentum: Riding the observed tendency for recent winners (the top performers over the past 6 to 12 months) to keep outperforming in the near term, as markets react slowly to new information.
3. Quality: Choosing companies with strong balance sheets, high Return on Equity (ROE), low debt-to-equity ratios, and stable, clean earnings.
4. Size (Small-Cap Premium): Historically, smaller, nimbler companies have delivered higher long-term growth than mature giants, though with bigger short-term price swings.
5. Low Volatility: A well-known market anomaly where steady, "boring" stocks with small price swings have delivered surprisingly strong risk-adjusted returns over decades, with smaller falls in market crashes.
Example: CA Jay shows Dia how a "Quality" filter weeds out companies with too much debt, keeping only financially strong market leaders in her portfolio.

LEARNING POINT 3: Indian Factor Indices (Rule-Based Indexing)
The National Stock Exchange of India (NSE) publishes factor indices that let retail investors access factor investing through low-cost index funds and ETFs. Examples include the NIFTY 200 Momentum 30, NIFTY 100 Quality 30, NIFTY 100 Low Volatility 30, and NIFTY 500 Value 50 indices. These indices rebalance automatically using transparent rules, typically every 6 months, removing human emotion and manager bias.
Example: Maanu invests in a NIFTY 100 Quality 30 index fund; every six months, the index automatically drops companies whose profitability or debt levels have worsened and replaces them with higher-quality firms.

LEARNING POINT 4: Multi-Factor Strategies & Factor Cycles
No single factor wins in every market. Momentum tends to thrive in strong bull markets but stumbles in sudden reversals; Value has tended to shine in early recoveries and inflationary periods; Low Volatility and Quality have tended to hold up better in crashes and uncertain, sideways markets. Blending two or more factors that don't move together (multi-factor investing, such as Quality + Low Volatility or Value + Momentum) can smooth out returns across different market conditions.
Example: Dia pairs a momentum factor fund with a low-volatility factor fund; when momentum takes a breather in choppy markets, her low-volatility holdings help steady the portfolio.

## Real-Life Fact
Economists Eugene Fama and Kenneth French transformed modern finance with research published in 1992–93 showing that company size and value, alongside overall market risk, help explain stock returns — their Three-Factor Model, later expanded to five factors. Fama shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2013 for his work on asset prices.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Believing that factor investing is a magic system that beats the standard market index every single month.
Correction: Individual factors can go through several years of underperformance; earning factor premiums needs patient, disciplined holding over 5- to 10-year horizons.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Factor investing combines the low-cost discipline of index funds with the potential to beat the market through rule-based screening.

D. Investing only in foreign government debt
Hint: It selects stocks systematically using proven measurable traits.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Factor investing uses rule-based filters (such as low valuation, strong balance sheets, or price trends) to build targeted index portfolios.

Q2. What defines the "Value factor" in stock screening?
A. Buying the most expensive stocks in the market
B. Selecting companies trading at low valuations (such as low P/E, low P/B, or high dividend yield) compared with their fundamentals
C. Buying stocks that have doubled in the last 2 days
D. Choosing companies that have no revenue
Hint: Value focuses on buying bargain-priced assets.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: The Value factor targets undervalued companies trading at a discount to their book value, earnings, or cash flows.

Q3. Which characteristics define the "Quality factor"?
A. Huge unpayable debt and falling revenues

D. Firms in business for less than 1 week
Hint: Quality focuses on balance-sheet strength, profitability, and low debt.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Quality indices screen for companies with high profitability (ROE/ROIC), low debt, and stable earnings.

Q4. What is the core idea behind the "Momentum factor"?

D. Stock prices only move on full-moon nights
Hint: Trends persist — recent winners tend to keep winning for a while.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Momentum investing captures trend continuation: stocks with strong relative performance over 6–12 months tend to keep doing well in the near term.

## Explore Further
Q1. What is the "low-volatility anomaly"?
A. Low-volatility stocks always lose 100% of their value
B. Portfolios of low-volatility or low-beta stocks have historically delivered better risk-adjusted returns than high-beta stocks, contradicting standard CAPM theory
C. Volatility can only be measured once every decade
D. Low-volatility stocks are not allowed to pay dividends
Hint: Standard theory says more risk means more return, but low-volatility stocks have done better on a risk-adjusted basis.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: The low-volatility anomaly is the finding that low-risk, low-beta stocks have produced risk-adjusted returns equal to or higher than high-risk stocks.

Q2. Which two factors did Eugene Fama and Kenneth French add to the market factor in their 1993 Three-Factor Model?
A. Weather and inflation
B. Size (SMB: Small Minus Big) and Value (HML: High Minus Low book-to-market)
C. Gold and real estate
D. Currency and tax rates
Hint: SMB captures the size premium; HML captures the value premium.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: The Fama-French Three-Factor Model extended CAPM by adding Size (SMB) and Value (HML) factors to explain differences in stock returns.

Q3. How often do standard NSE factor indices (such as NIFTY 200 Momentum 30) typically rebalance?
A. Every 10 seconds
B. Semi-annually (twice a year, e.g. in June and December)
C. Once every 50 years
D. Never
Hint: Factor indices update their constituents every six months.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Most NSE factor indices are reviewed and rebalanced twice a year using their predefined rules.

Q4. What is "factor crowding", and what risk does it pose?
A. Too many traders standing inside a stock exchange room
B. When large amounts of money chase the same factor strategy, valuations get stretched and sharp, simultaneous falls can follow when investors rush out
C. A software bug in bank computers
D. A government tax on mutual fund units
Hint: When a factor trade gets overcrowded, sudden exits cause sharp sell-offs.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Factor crowding happens when too much money flows into the same factor profile, shrinking its future premium and causing volatile sell-offs when investors unwind.

Q5. What is the main benefit of a multi-factor strategy (such as combining Quality and Value)?
A. It promises 100% tax exemption on all income
B. It reduces the ups and downs of any single factor by blending complementary factors that don't move together
C. It removes the need for a Demat account
D. It fixes stock prices at a constant rate forever
Hint: Combining factors that behave differently smooths performance across market cycles.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Multi-factor investing diversifies across different return drivers (e.g. Value and Quality), softening the long periods when any one factor underperforms.
