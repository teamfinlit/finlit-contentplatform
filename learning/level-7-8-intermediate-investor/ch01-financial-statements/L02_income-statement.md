# Income Statement
**Level:** 7-8 Intermediate Investor | **Chapter:** 1 - Financial Statements | **Topic:** 2

## Key Concepts
- The Profit & Loss Statement
- Revenue
- COGS
- Gross Profit
- Net Profit
- Gross Margin
- Operating Margin
- Net Margin

## Quick Spark
The income statement (or Profit & Loss statement) shows how much a company earned and spent over a period of time. Revenue minus costs gives you profit at different stages — gross profit, operating profit, and finally net profit, the 'bottom line.' Margins turn these into percentages, showing how efficiently a company converts sales into profit.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: The Profit & Loss Statement — Tracking Performance Over Time
Unlike a balance sheet's single-moment snapshot, the Profit & Loss (P&L) statement — also called the income statement — covers a stretch of time, typically a quarter or a full financial year, showing everything the company earned and spent during that window. It works by starting with total revenue and subtracting different categories of costs step by step, arriving at several profit figures along the way rather than just one final number. This structure exists because a business's performance isn't just "did it make money" — it's "how did it make money, and where did the costs eat into it." So the P&L answers a very different question than the balance sheet: not "what does the company own right now," but "how did the company actually perform over this period?"
Example: CA Jay explains to interns: "If the balance sheet is a photograph, the income statement is a movie — it shows what happened between two dates, not just where things stand today."

LEARNING POINT 2: Revenue — The Top Line
Revenue — often called the "top line" because it sits at the very top of the income statement — represents everything the company earned from selling its goods or services during the period, before any expenses are deducted. It works as the starting point for every profit calculation that follows: nothing else can be calculated until revenue is established first. Revenue matters because it reflects market demand — how much customers were willing to pay for what the company offers — making it the clearest signal of sales performance, independent of how efficiently costs are managed. So while revenue alone doesn't tell you if a company is profitable, strong and growing revenue is usually the first sign a business has something people want to buy.
Example: If Maanu's family stationery shop sells ₹12,00,000 worth of notebooks and pens in a year, that ₹12,00,000 is their revenue — before subtracting the cost of goods or any other expenses.

LEARNING POINT 3: COGS (Cost of Goods Sold) — The Direct Cost of Making What You Sell
COGS includes only the costs directly tied to producing or purchasing what was sold — raw materials, direct labour, and manufacturing costs — but excludes indirect expenses like marketing, rent, or office salaries. It works as the first cost subtracted from revenue because it represents the most fundamental expense a business faces: you generally can't sell a product without first paying to make or acquire it. COGS matters because it isolates the core cost of the product itself from all other costs of running a business. So COGS answers a very specific question: "for every rupee of revenue, how much did it directly cost just to create or obtain the product being sold?"
Example: For Maanu's family shop, COGS would be what they paid the wholesaler for the notebooks and pens themselves — not the shop's rent or the shopkeeper's salary.

LEARNING POINT 4: Gross Profit — What's Left After Direct Costs
Gross Profit is calculated simply as Revenue minus COGS, showing how much money is left after paying for what was directly needed to produce or acquire the goods sold. It works as the first profit checkpoint on the income statement — before any other business expenses like rent, salaries, or marketing are even considered. Gross Profit matters because it reveals how much "cushion" a business has from its core product economics alone, separate from how well it manages its broader operations. So a company with strong gross profit has room to cover its other expenses and still turn a profit, while one with thin gross profit is financially squeezed before overhead costs are even factored in.
Example: If Maanu's family shop earns ₹12,00,000 in revenue and spent ₹7,00,000 on COGS, their gross profit is ₹5,00,000 — the amount left to cover rent, salaries, and other costs.

LEARNING POINT 5: Net Profit — The Bottom Line
Net Profit, commonly called the "bottom line" because it's the final figure at the bottom of the income statement, is calculated as Revenue minus every single expense the company incurred — COGS, operating expenses, interest, taxes, and everything else. It works as the ultimate performance summary: the single number capturing whether, after accounting for absolutely everything, the business actually made money during the period. Net Profit matters because a company can have strong revenue and even solid gross profit, yet still lose money overall if operating costs, interest, or taxes consume everything else. So while revenue shows sales strength and gross profit shows product economics, net profit is the number that tells you whether the whole business was actually worth running that period.
Example: After subtracting rent, salaries, taxes, and all other costs from their ₹5,00,000 gross profit, Maanu's family might find their actual net profit for the year is closer to ₹1,80,000.

LEARNING POINT 6: Gross Margin, Operating Margin & Net Margin — Turning Profit Into Percentages
Margins convert raw profit numbers into percentages by dividing each profit figure by revenue and multiplying by 100 — Gross Margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100, Operating Margin = Operating Profit ÷ Revenue × 100 (operating profit being profit after regular running costs but before interest and tax), and Net Margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100. They work by neutralising the effect of company size — a small business earning ₹50,000 profit and a giant company earning ₹50 crore profit can't be fairly compared in rupees, but their margins (say, both at 15%) show they're equally efficient at converting sales into profit. Margins matter because higher percentages generally indicate a company keeps a larger share of each rupee of sales as profit — but margins should be compared with industry peers, since a software company and a grocery retailer naturally operate at very different typical margin levels. So margins turn "how much profit" into "how efficiently," usually the more useful question for comparing businesses of different sizes.
Example: CA Jay compares two clients: one with ₹10,00,000 net profit on ₹1,00,00,000 revenue (10% net margin) and another with ₹10,00,000 net profit on ₹40,00,000 revenue (25% net margin) — the second is converting sales into profit far more efficiently.

## Real-Life Fact
Under Indian company law and SEBI listing regulations, publicly listed companies must publish quarterly income statements (often called quarterly results), which is why financial news frequently reports on companies "beating" or "missing" revenue and profit expectations every three months.
Software and IT services companies often report high gross margins (sometimes 60-70%+) because their COGS is relatively low compared to manufacturing businesses, which typically have much thinner gross margins due to raw material and production costs — this is why comparing margins should generally stay within the same industry.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Assuming a company with higher revenue is automatically more profitable or a "better" business than one with lower revenue.
Correction: Revenue only measures sales size, not efficiency or actual profit — a company with lower revenue but a higher net margin can generate more profit per rupee of sales, and can even be the more attractive business overall.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Revenue tells you how much a company sold; margins tell you how well it kept what it earned — always look at both before judging a business's performance.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What is another common name for the income statement?
A. Balance Sheet
B. Profit & Loss (P&L) Statement
C. Cash Flow Statement
D. Statement of Assets
Hint: Recall the alternate name mentioned early in the lesson.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: The income statement is also widely known as the Profit & Loss (P&L) Statement, since it summarizes revenue, costs, and resulting profit over a period.

Q2. What does COGS represent?
A. All company expenses, including marketing and rent
B. The direct costs of producing or acquiring the goods sold
C. The company's total revenue
D. Interest paid on loans
Hint: Focus on "direct" costs tied specifically to the product.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: COGS includes only the direct costs of producing or acquiring what was sold — like raw materials and direct labour — excluding indirect costs like marketing or office rent.

Q3. A company has revenue of ₹50,00,000 and COGS of ₹30,00,000. What is its gross profit and gross margin?
A. Gross profit ₹20,00,000; Gross margin 40%
B. Gross profit ₹30,00,000; Gross margin 60%
C. Gross profit ₹80,00,000; Gross margin 160%
D. Gross profit ₹20,00,000; Gross margin 60%
Hint: Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS; Gross Margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Gross Profit = ₹50,00,000 − ₹30,00,000 = ₹20,00,000. Gross Margin = ₹20,00,000 ÷ ₹50,00,000 × 100 = 40%.

Q4. Why might two companies with identical net profit in rupees have very different net margins?
A. Net margin depends only on the company's location
B. They likely have different revenue amounts, since net margin is profit relative to revenue, not an absolute rupee figure
C. Net margin is unrelated to revenue or profit
D. It's impossible for two companies to have different net margins with the same profit
Hint: Recall that margin is a ratio, not a rupee amount.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Since net margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100, two companies with the same rupee profit but different revenue levels will have different net margins.

Q5. Company A (a software firm) has a gross margin of 65%. Company B (a furniture manufacturer) has a gross margin of 25%. Should an investor conclude Company B is a poorly run business compared to Company A?
A. Yes, always — lower margin always means worse management
B. Not necessarily — different industries naturally have different typical margin levels, so comparisons should be industry-relative, not absolute
C. Yes, because all companies should have identical margins
D. No comparison is possible between any two companies ever
Hint: Recall why margins should be compared within the same industry.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Margins vary naturally by industry — asset-light software businesses typically post far higher gross margins than manufacturing businesses with heavy raw material and production costs.

Q2. Why do software and IT services companies typically report much higher gross margins than manufacturing companies?
A. Software companies are exempt from all costs
B. Their COGS is relatively low compared to manufacturers, who face significant raw material and production costs
C. Manufacturing companies never generate any profit
D. Gross margin doesn't apply to software companies
Hint: Recall the industry comparison in the Real-Life Fact.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Software businesses often have low direct costs relative to revenue, leading to structurally higher gross margins than manufacturers who must pay for materials and production processes.

Q3. What is "operating profit," and how does it differ from net profit?
A. They are exactly the same thing
B. Operating profit is profit after regular running costs but before interest and tax, while net profit accounts for interest and tax as well
C. Operating profit includes only revenue, with no costs subtracted
D. Operating profit is always higher than revenue
Hint: Recall how Operating Margin was described relative to Net Margin.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Operating profit captures how the core business is performing before financial costs like interest and taxes are applied, while net profit is the final figure after absolutely everything has been subtracted.

Q4. If a growing company's revenue increases significantly but its net margin shrinks at the same time, what might this suggest for deeper research?
A. Nothing — margins are irrelevant if revenue grows
B. Costs may be growing faster than revenue, worth investigating (e.g., rising raw material costs, heavy marketing spend, or inefficiency)
C. The company is definitely about to go bankrupt
D. The company has stopped selling products
Hint: Think about what a shrinking margin despite rising revenue implies about the relationship between costs and sales.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Rising revenue with shrinking margins often signals that costs are growing even faster than sales, a common area analysts investigate further.

Q5. Why might an investor prefer a smaller company with a 25% net margin over a larger company with a 5% net margin, all else being equal?

C. Net margin has no bearing on investment decisions
D. Revenue size is the only factor that matters
Hint: Recall the Common Mistake about revenue size versus profitability.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: A higher net margin indicates the company retains more profit per rupee of sales, reflecting better cost control, pricing power, or operational efficiency.
