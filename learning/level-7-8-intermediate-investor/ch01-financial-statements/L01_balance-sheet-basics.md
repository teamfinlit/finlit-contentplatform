# Balance Sheet Basics
**Level:** 7-8 Intermediate Investor | **Chapter:** 1 - Financial Statements | **Topic:** 1

- Assets

- Current ratio

## Quick Spark
A balance sheet is a snapshot of what a company owns (assets) versus what it owes (liabilities) and what's left for owners (equity) — and it always balances, because Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Ratios like the current ratio and debt-to-equity ratio help you judge whether a company is financially healthy or overstretched.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: The Balance Sheet — A Financial Snapshot
A balance sheet lists everything a company owns and owes on a single specific date, structured as a simple, unbreakable equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. It works because every asset in a business is funded by one of two sources — money borrowed from others (liabilities) or money invested by owners (equity) — so the two sides must always be equal, hence the name "balance" sheet. This equation holds because accounting is built on double-entry bookkeeping: every rupee of value on one side has to be accounted for by where it came from on the other. So a balance sheet answers a very specific question at a single point in time — "if we added up everything this company owns and subtracted everything it owes, what would be left for the owners?"
Example: CA Jay tells his interns: "Think of a balance sheet like a photograph, not a movie — it shows the company's financial position on one exact date, not how it changed over the year."

LEARNING POINT 2: Assets — What the Company Owns
Assets are resources the company owns or controls — cash in the bank, inventory in a warehouse, property, equipment, and money owed to it by customers. They represent the company's total resource base — the tools and reserves it uses to run and grow the business. Assets exist on the balance sheet because a company needs resources to operate: it can't sell products without inventory, run a factory without machinery, or pay bills without cash. So the asset side answers "what does this company have to work with?" — a company with strong, growing assets generally has more capacity to produce, sell, and earn.
Example: When Maanu's family runs their small stationery shop, their assets include the cash in the register, the notebooks and pens on the shelves, and the shop's furniture.

LEARNING POINT 3: Liabilities — What the Company Owes
Liabilities are the company's obligations to others — bank loans, unpaid supplier bills (accounts payable), and bonds issued to investors that must eventually be repaid. They work as claims against the company's assets — before owners get anything, lenders and suppliers must be paid what they're owed. Liabilities exist because most businesses don't fund everything with their own money; they borrow to grow faster than their own cash would allow, accepting the obligation to repay in exchange for extra resources today. So the liability side answers "who has a claim on this company's assets, and how much?" — and too many liabilities relative to assets can signal financial strain.
Example: CA Jay's client took a bank loan to buy new machinery for her factory — that loan appears as a liability until it's fully repaid.

LEARNING POINT 4: Equity — The Owner's Stake
Equity is calculated as Assets minus Liabilities — it represents the portion of the company that genuinely belongs to its owners or shareholders, free of any debt claims. It works as the "residual" value: if the company sold every asset and paid off every liability today, whatever cash remained would belong to the equity holders. Equity exists as a concept because ownership and debt are fundamentally different — a lender has a fixed claim (get repaid a set amount) while an owner has a variable claim (get whatever's left). So growing equity over time is usually a sign that a company is building real, debt-free value for its owners.
Example: If Maanu's family shop has ₹5,00,000 in assets and ₹2,00,000 in liabilities (a business loan), their equity — their true ownership stake — is ₹3,00,000.

LEARNING POINT 5: Current Assets vs Fixed Assets — Time Horizon Matters
Current assets are things expected to convert into cash within a year — cash itself, and receivables (money customers owe, expected soon) — while fixed assets are longer-term holdings like property and machinery used for years, not months. This split works because not all value is equally "usable" right now — a factory building can't pay next month's electricity bill, but cash and near-term receivables can. This distinction matters because it lets analysts judge short-term financial flexibility separately from long-term productive capacity. So separating current from fixed assets really answers: "how much of what this company owns could become spendable cash soon, versus how much is tied up for the long haul?"
Example: CA Jay explains: "The cash in the shop's register is a current asset — it could be spent tomorrow. The shop's building is a fixed asset — it'll still be there in ten years."

LEARNING POINT 6: Current Ratio — Testing Short-Term Health
The current ratio is Current Assets divided by Current Liabilities, showing how many rupees of short-term assets exist for every rupee of short-term debt due soon. It works as a quick health check: a ratio above 1.5 generally suggests the company holds comfortably more short-term resources than short-term obligations, indicating good ability to pay bills and short-term loans without a cash crunch. This ratio matters because a company can look profitable overall while still struggling to pay this month's bills if its cash and near-term assets are too thin relative to what's due soon. So it essentially asks: "if all short-term debts came due right now, could this company comfortably cover them?"
Example: A company with ₹15,00,000 in current assets and ₹8,00,000 in current liabilities has a current ratio of 1.875 — generally considered healthy.

LEARNING POINT 7: Debt-to-Equity Ratio — Testing How the Company Is Financed
The debt-to-equity ratio is Total Liabilities divided by Total Equity, showing how many rupees of debt exist for every rupee the owners have invested. It works as a leverage check: a ratio below 1 generally means the company relies more on owner funding than borrowed money, viewed as more conservatively financed. This ratio matters because higher debt increases both potential returns and potential risk — interest must be paid regardless of performance, so a heavily indebted company can be squeezed hard during a downturn. So it essentially asks: "if this business hits a rough patch, how much of its structure is fixed debt obligations versus flexible owner capital?"
Example: A company with ₹40,00,000 in liabilities and ₹60,00,000 in equity has a debt-to-equity ratio of about 0.67 — a relatively conservative financing structure.

## Real-Life Fact
SEBI requires all publicly listed Indian companies to publish financial results every quarter, a balance sheet every six months, and a full audited annual report every year — all freely available to any investor on the BSE and NSE websites or on the company's own investor relations page.
Analysts commonly treat a current ratio between 1.5 and 3 as a healthy range — a ratio far above 3 can sometimes indicate a company is holding too much idle cash instead of putting it to productive use.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Assuming a company with a very high current ratio (e.g., 8 or 10) is automatically in excellent financial shape.
Correction: An unusually high current ratio can actually signal inefficiency — the company may be sitting on too much idle cash or unsold inventory instead of reinvesting it to grow the business, so extremely high isn't automatically "better."

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: A balance sheet always balances because everything a company owns is paid for by either debt or ownership — so reading Assets = Liabilities + Equity tells you not just what a company has, but who really owns it.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What does the balance sheet equation state?
A. Revenue = Expenses + Profit
B. Assets = Liabilities + Equity
C. Cash In = Cash Out
D. Profit = Revenue − Expenses
Hint: Recall the equation that always balances.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: The balance sheet is built on the equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity, meaning everything a company owns is funded either by debt or by ownership.

Q2. Which of the following is a current asset?
A. Factory building
B. Heavy machinery
C. Cash in the bank
D. A 10-year office lease
Hint: Think about what can be converted to cash within a year.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: Cash is a current asset because it's already liquid or convertible within a year, unlike fixed assets like buildings or machinery, held for the long term.

Q3. A company has current assets of ₹20,00,000 and current liabilities of ₹10,00,000. What is its current ratio, and is it generally considered healthy?
A. 0.5, unhealthy
B. 2.0, generally healthy
C. 10, unhealthy
D. 200, generally healthy
Hint: Divide current assets by current liabilities.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: ₹20,00,000 ÷ ₹10,00,000 = 2.0, above the 1.5 threshold generally considered healthy, meaning likely enough short-term assets to cover short-term obligations.

Q4. Why might a company with a debt-to-equity ratio of 3 be considered riskier than one with a ratio of 0.5?
A. Because a higher ratio means the company has no assets at all
B. Because a higher ratio means the company relies heavily on debt, which must be repaid regardless of business performance
C. Because a higher ratio always means the company is more profitable
D. Because ratios above 1 are illegal
Hint: Think about what debt obligates a company to do, even in bad years.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: A high debt-to-equity ratio means heavy dependence on borrowed money; since interest and principal must be paid regardless of performance, this increases financial risk during downturns.

Q5. Two companies have identical assets of ₹1 crore. Company X has ₹80 lakh liabilities and ₹20 lakh equity; Company Y has ₹20 lakh liabilities and ₹80 lakh equity. Which is more conservatively financed?
A. Company X, because it has more total assets
B. Company Y, because it relies far less on debt relative to owner capital
C. Both are equally conservative since total assets are the same
D. Company X, because higher liabilities always mean more resources
Hint: Compare each company's liabilities to its equity.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Company Y has a much lower debt-to-equity ratio (0.25 vs. 4.0 for Company X), relying far more on owner capital — a more conservative, typically safer financing structure.

## Explore Further
Q1. Which Indian regulator mandates that publicly listed companies file audited financial statements, including balance sheets, on a regular basis?
A. RBI
B. SEBI
C. NPCI
D. IRDAI
Hint: Recall the regulator mentioned in the Real-Life Fact.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) mandates periodic financial disclosures, including audited balance sheets, from all publicly listed companies to protect investor interests.

Q2. What accounting system underlies the balance sheet equation always balancing (Assets = Liabilities + Equity)?
A. Single-entry bookkeeping
B. Double-entry bookkeeping
C. Cash-only accounting
D. Random-entry accounting
Hint: Think about how every transaction affects two sides of the equation.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Double-entry bookkeeping records every transaction in at least two accounts (a debit and a credit), which is precisely why the balance sheet's two sides always stay equal.

Q3. Why might analysts compare a company's current ratio and debt-to-equity ratio to other companies in the same industry, rather than across different industries?
A. Because ratios are meaningless outside comparison
B. Because "normal" ratio levels vary significantly by industry (e.g., capital-heavy industries often carry more debt than software companies)
C. Because all industries have identical financial structures
D. Because government regulations require industry-only comparisons
Hint: Think about how a capital-intensive business like manufacturing differs structurally from a low-asset business like software.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Different industries naturally carry different typical levels of debt and liquidity — a capital-intensive manufacturer often carries more debt to fund machinery than an asset-light software company, so meaningful comparisons need an industry-relative context.

Q4. A company holding an extremely high current ratio (e.g., 12) might actually be criticized by investors for what reason?
A. Holding too much idle cash or unsold inventory instead of reinvesting for growth
B. Being at high risk of bankruptcy
C. Having illegal accounting practices
D. Having too little cash
Hint: Recall the Common Mistake about unusually high ratios.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: An extremely high current ratio can indicate inefficient capital use — resources sitting idle instead of being reinvested to grow the business — rather than being purely a sign of strength.

Q5. If a company wants to lower its debt-to-equity ratio to appear more conservatively financed, which action would directly help?
A. Taking on more bank loans
B. Repaying existing debt or raising more equity capital from investors
C. Increasing current liabilities
D. Reducing its cash holdings
Hint: Think about what changes the numerator or denominator of the ratio in the right direction.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Reducing total liabilities (by repaying debt) or increasing total equity (by raising capital from investors) both directly lower the debt-to-equity ratio, signaling a more conservative financing structure.
