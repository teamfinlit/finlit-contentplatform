# Cash Flow Statement
**Level:** 7-8 Intermediate Investor | **Chapter:** 1 - Financial Statements | **Topic:** 3

## Key Concepts
- Following the Cash
- Operating
- Investing
- Financing
- Free Cash Flow (FCF)
- Cash vs profit

## Quick Spark
The cash flow statement tracks actual cash moving in and out of a company across three activities — operating, investing, and financing. A business can look profitable on paper yet still run out of cash; Free Cash Flow, what's left after essential spending, is often the truest test of financial health. As the saying goes: profits are opinions, cash is a fact.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: Following the Cash — What the Cash Flow Statement Shows
The cash flow statement records real cash transactions — money that physically entered or left the company's bank accounts — over a period, separated into three distinct sections: operating, investing, and financing. It works differently from the income statement because it ignores accounting adjustments like credit sales not yet collected, and instead tracks only when cash actually changes hands. This exists because accounting profit and actual cash are calculated using different rules — a sale can be "recorded" as revenue the moment it's invoiced, even if the customer hasn't paid yet. So the cash flow statement answers a very specific and practical question: "did cash actually go up or down, and because of what kind of activity?"
Example: CA Jay tells clients: "The income statement can show a great year on paper, but the cash flow statement tells you whether the company could actually pay its staff's salaries this month."

LEARNING POINT 2: Operating Activities — Cash From the Core Business
The operating section captures cash flows tied directly to running the core business — cash received from customers, cash paid to suppliers, employee salaries, and other everyday operating costs. It works as the most closely watched section because it shows whether the company's fundamental business is generating real cash, independent of one-off asset sales or borrowing. This matters most because a business that can't generate positive operating cash flow from its core activities is ultimately not sustainable, no matter how it might be propped up temporarily by loans or asset sales. So operating cash flow answers: "is the actual business, on its own, bringing in more cash than it's spending to run?"
Example: For Maanu's family shop, operating cash flow would be the cash collected from customers buying notebooks, minus the cash paid to suppliers and staff that month.

LEARNING POINT 3: Investing Activities — Cash From Buying and Selling Assets
The investing section tracks cash spent on acquiring long-term assets — like machinery, property, or other companies — and cash received from selling such assets or investments. It works as a lens into how the company is growing or shrinking its productive capacity: heavy spending here (a cash outflow) often signals expansion, while cash inflows might mean the company is selling off assets, sometimes to raise money in tough times. This section matters because it's normal, even healthy, for a growing company to show negative investing cash flow — spending cash to build future capacity is often a good sign, unlike negative operating cash flow. So investing cash flow answers: "how much is the company putting into (or pulling out of) its long-term growth engine?"
Example: When a factory buys a new production line for ₹50,00,000, that entire amount shows as a cash outflow under investing activities that year.

LEARNING POINT 4: Financing Activities — Cash From Borrowing, Repaying, and Paying Owners
The financing section records cash received from borrowing (loans, bonds) or issuing new shares, and cash paid out for repaying loans, buying back shares, or paying dividends to shareholders. It works as a picture of the company's relationship with its lenders and owners — how much fresh capital is coming in, and how much is being returned. This matters because financing activity can temporarily mask problems elsewhere — a company might show healthy overall cash flow simply because it borrowed heavily, even while its actual operating business is struggling. So financing cash flow answers: "how is the company managing its relationship with lenders and owners — raising capital, repaying it, or rewarding shareholders?"
Example: When a company pays out ₹10,00,000 in dividends to shareholders or repays ₹20,00,000 of a bank loan, both appear as cash outflows under financing activities.

LEARNING POINT 5: Cash vs Profit — Why "Profitable" Doesn't Always Mean "Cash-Rich"
Under accrual accounting — the standard method used for the income statement — revenue is recorded when a sale happens, not necessarily when cash is actually received, meaning a company can book large "profits" from sales that customers haven't paid for yet. This creates a real gap because the company still has bills, salaries, and loan payments due in actual cash, regardless of what its income statement shows as profit. This happens because profit is an accounting concept measuring performance using specific rules, while cash is a simple, literal fact about what's actually in the bank right now. So the lesson is that profit and cash are related but different measures — strong reported profit offers no guarantee that the cash to actually operate is available.
Example: A company might report ₹1 crore in net profit for the year, but if most of that came from unpaid customer invoices, its actual bank balance could be dangerously low.

LEARNING POINT 6: Free Cash Flow (FCF) — The Real Health Check
Free Cash Flow is calculated as Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditure (the money spent maintaining or upgrading long-term assets), showing how much genuinely spare cash the business generates after keeping its operations running. It works as arguably the purest health indicator because it strips out accounting adjustments and shows real, spendable cash left over — money the company could use to pay down debt, reward shareholders, or reinvest, without needing to borrow further. FCF matters because a company can show strong operating cash flow yet still have little left over once it accounts for the ongoing capital spending required just to maintain its equipment and facilities. So Free Cash Flow answers the ultimate practical question: "after keeping the business running, how much real spare cash is actually left?"
Example: If a company generates ₹2 crore in operating cash flow but spends ₹1.5 crore on capital expenditure to maintain its factories, its Free Cash Flow is just ₹50 lakh — a much smaller, more revealing number than the headline operating figure.

## Real-Life Fact
Warren Buffett, one of the world's most well-known long-term investors, has repeatedly emphasized favouring companies with consistent, strong free cash flow generation as a key factor in his investment approach, since it reflects genuine cash-generating ability rather than accounting figures alone.
Corporate bankruptcies frequently occur even for companies reporting accounting profits in prior periods, because a business fundamentally fails when it runs out of actual cash to pay its obligations — not necessarily when its income statement turns negative.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Assuming a company reporting strong net profit on its income statement must also be financially safe and cash-healthy.
Correction: Profit is an accounting measure that can include revenue not yet collected in cash, so a profitable company can still face a real cash shortage — always check the cash flow statement alongside the income statement before judging financial health.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Profits are opinions shaped by accounting rules, but cash is a fact — a business survives on the cash it actually has, not the profit it reports on paper.

## Try It in Real Life
Find the latest annual cash flow statement of any publicly listed company (search the company name plus "cash flow statement"). Identify the cash flow from operating, investing, and financing activities, then calculate an approximate Free Cash Flow (Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditure, often listed under investing activities). Note whether the company's FCF is positive or negative and consider why.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What are the three main sections of a cash flow statement?
A. Assets, Liabilities, Equity
B. Operating, Investing, Financing
C. Revenue, COGS, Profit
D. Current, Fixed, Total
Hint: Recall the three categories the cash flow statement is divided into.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: The cash flow statement is divided into Operating, Investing, and Financing activities, each capturing a different type of cash movement.

Q2. Which section of the cash flow statement captures cash from a company's normal day-to-day business?
A. Investing activities
B. Financing activities
C. Operating activities
D. None of the above
Hint: Think about which section reflects the core, everyday business.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: Operating activities capture cash generated or used by the company's core, everyday business operations — the most closely watched section of the statement.

Q3. A company reports ₹80,00,000 in net profit for the year but has ₹60,00,000 tied up in unpaid customer invoices. What does this suggest about its actual cash position?
A. The company definitely has ₹80,00,000 in cash available
B. The company's actual cash position could be much lower than its reported profit suggests
C. Unpaid invoices have no impact on cash flow
D. The company's profit figure is automatically incorrect
Hint: Recall the difference between accrual-based profit and actual cash.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Since revenue can be recorded before cash is actually received, a company with large unpaid invoices can show strong profit while having significantly less actual cash on hand.

Q4. Why is negative investing cash flow not necessarily a bad sign for a growing company?
A. Negative numbers are always meaningless
B. It often reflects healthy spending on long-term assets like machinery or property to support future growth
C. It means the company is losing money overall
D. Investing cash flow doesn't affect a company's finances
Hint: Think about what spending on long-term assets usually signals for an expanding business.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Negative investing cash flow frequently reflects a company investing in its future capacity, often a healthy sign for a growing business, unlike negative operating cash flow.

Q5. Company X has ₹3 crore in operating cash flow and ₹2.8 crore in capital expenditure. Company Y has ₹1.5 crore in operating cash flow and ₹20 lakh in capital expenditure. Which company has the healthier Free Cash Flow, and why?
A. Company X, because its operating cash flow is higher
B. Company Y, because its Free Cash Flow (₹1.3 crore) is much higher than Company X's (₹20 lakh), despite lower operating cash flow
C. Both are equal since operating cash flow is what matters most
D. Neither has any Free Cash Flow
Hint: Calculate FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure for both.
Correct Answer: B
Concept / Fact Explanation: Company X's FCF is ₹3 crore − ₹2.8 crore = ₹20 lakh, while Company Y's FCF is ₹1.5 crore − ₹20 lakh = ₹1.3 crore — Company Y retains far more genuinely spare cash despite lower headline operating cash flow.

Q2. Why do companies sometimes go bankrupt despite reporting accounting profits in prior periods?
A. Bankruptcy is unrelated to cash availability

Fact / Concept Explanation: Since obligations like salaries, loan payments, and supplier bills must be paid in actual cash, a company can be profitable on paper yet still collapse if it genuinely runs out of cash to meet those obligations.

Q3. What accounting method causes the timing gap between when revenue is recorded and when cash is actually received?
A. Cash-basis accounting
B. Accrual accounting
C. Zero-based accounting
D. Government accounting
Hint: Recall the accounting method used for the income statement, discussed in the Cash vs Profit learning point.
Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Accrual accounting records revenue when a sale occurs (e.g., when an invoice is raised), not necessarily when cash is received, which is precisely why profit and cash can diverge.

Q4. If a company consistently shows strong operating cash flow but persistently negative free cash flow due to very high capital expenditure, what might this suggest for further research?

Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Persistently negative FCF despite strong operating cash flow could reflect aggressive expansion or unusually heavy asset maintenance needs — either way, a signal for deeper research rather than an automatic red flag.

Q5. Why might an analyst specifically check the financing activities section before concluding a company's overall cash position is healthy?

Correct Answer: B
Fact / Concept Explanation: Strong total cash flow can sometimes be driven mainly by fresh borrowing or share issuance rather than genuine business performance, so analysts check whether operating cash flow is genuinely healthy on its own, not just propped up by financing inflows.
