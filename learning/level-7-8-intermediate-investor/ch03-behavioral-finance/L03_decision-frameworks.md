# Decision Frameworks
**Level:** 7-8 Intermediate Investor | **Chapter:** 3 - Behavioral Finance | **Topic:** 3

## Key Concepts
- Smart Investment Decision-Making
- Quantitative Checks

## Quick Spark
Good investing isn't about gut feeling — it's about running every idea through a checklist before you buy: does it fit your circle of competence, your allocation plan, your time horizon, and do you have an exit plan? If you can't answer the numbers, index funds are the honest fallback.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: The Checklist Approach
Instead of deciding to invest based on excitement or a tip, a checklist approach means running every potential investment through the same set of standard questions every single time — turning a subjective, emotional decision into a repeatable, disciplined process.

LEARNING POINT 2: Circle of Competence
"Is this within my circle of competence?" asks whether you actually understand the business, the industry, and how it makes money — not just its ticker symbol or its recent price chart. Investing in what you genuinely understand means you can judge whether news about the business is actually good or bad news, instead of reacting blindly.

LEARNING POINT 3: Fit With Your Allocation Plan
Before buying, check whether this specific investment fits the asset-allocation plan you've already set (equity/debt/gold mix, sector limits, single-stock limits) — an exciting opportunity that blows past your 10%-per-stock or 25%-per-sector rule isn't actually a good decision, however attractive it looks in isolation.

LEARNING POINT 4: Time Horizon
"What's my time horizon?" matters because different goals need different vehicles — money needed in 6 months for a specific goal shouldn't sit in volatile equities, where a bad month could force a sale at a loss right when the cash is needed; only money that won't be needed for several years belongs in equity.

LEARNING POINT 5: Exit Strategy — Decided Before Buying
Setting price targets and exit conditions before buying — not after — removes the temptation to keep moving the goalposts once emotion is involved. Deciding "I will sell if it reaches X, or if it falls by Y%" while calm is far more reliable than deciding live, mid-swing.

LEARNING POINT 6: Quantitative Checks — Reading the Numbers
Several concrete, checkable numbers back up a decision:
- The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio compares a stock's price to its per-share earnings, and comparing it against the industry average shows whether it is priced reasonably relative to similar businesses.
- The Debt-to-Equity ratio (preferably below 1) shows how much a company relies on borrowed money versus its own capital — heavily indebted companies carry more risk in a downturn.
- Revenue growth checks whether the business is genuinely growing its sales year over year, not just its stock price.
- Free cash flow — the actual cash left after running and investing in the business — being positive and growing shows the company generates real, usable money rather than only paper profit.

LEARNING POINT 7: The Honest Fallback — Index Funds
If an investor genuinely cannot answer these checklist and quantitative questions for a specific stock, the framework's honest advice is to stick to index funds — funds that simply track a broad market index — rather than gambling on individual stock selection without the tools to evaluate it properly.
Example: CA Jay, 47, is asked by a colleague to invest in a hot new stock. He can't confidently answer the debt-to-equity or free-cash-flow questions about the company, so he sticks to his index fund instead of chasing the tip.

## Real-Life Fact
SEBI-registered index funds and ETFs that track benchmarks such as the Nifty 50 or Sensex have grown into some of the most widely recommended default options in Indian retail investing, precisely for investors who want equity exposure without needing to individually evaluate every company's P/E ratio, debt levels, or cash flow.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Buying a stock because "everyone says it's going to grow" without checking a single number — P/E ratio, debt levels, or revenue trend.
Correction: A checklist exists precisely to separate a real investment decision from a hunch; if you can't answer the basic quantitative questions, that itself is useful information telling you to stick to a simpler, broader option like an index fund.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: If you can't answer the checklist, the honest answer is an index fund — not a guess dressed up as conviction.

## Try It in Real Life
Pick any company you know well as a customer (a brand you use often). Try to find its P/E ratio and compare it to its industry average, and check whether you could honestly answer all five checklist questions (competence, allocation fit, time horizon, exit strategy, and the numbers) about it before deciding whether you'd be comfortable investing.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What does "circle of competence" mean in the investment checklist?
A. Whether you genuinely understand the business and how it makes money
B. Whether the company's logo is well designed
C. Whether the stock is the most talked-about one on social media
D. Whether the company is headquartered in a major city
Hint: Think "understanding," not "popularity."
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Circle of competence asks whether you truly understand how the business operates and earns money, not how well-known or trendy it is.

Q2. Why should an exit strategy be decided BEFORE buying an investment, according to the lesson?
A. Deciding calmly in advance avoids emotional, in-the-moment decisions once the investment is already owned
B. It is a legal requirement under Indian securities law
C. It guarantees the investment will only ever go up
D. It removes the need to ever sell the investment
Hint: Think about when emotions are strongest.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Setting exit conditions while calm removes the temptation to make an emotional, in-the-moment decision after already owning the investment.

Q3. A company has a Debt-to-Equity ratio of 2.5. According to the lesson's quantitative check, how should this be viewed?
A. As a warning sign, since a ratio below 1 is generally preferred
B. As an excellent sign of financial strength
C. As irrelevant to investment decisions
D. As proof the company's revenue is growing
Hint: The lesson prefers debt-to-equity under 1.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: A Debt-to-Equity ratio well above the preferred level of 1 signals heavier reliance on borrowed money, which is a risk flag.

Q4. Why does the lesson recommend checking free cash flow rather than just looking at reported profit?
A. Free cash flow shows the actual usable cash the business generates after running and investing in itself, which can differ from accounting profit

D. Free cash flow has no connection to a company's financial health
Hint: Think "real cash" versus "paper profit."
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Free cash flow reflects real, usable cash after operating and investing costs, which can differ meaningfully from reported accounting profit.

Q5. An investor cannot confidently answer the checklist questions (competence, allocation fit, time horizon, exit strategy, and the quantitative checks) for a specific stock. What does the lesson recommend?

Concept / Fact Explanation: When the checklist can't be honestly completed, the lesson's recommended fallback is a broad index fund rather than an individual stock pick.

## Explore Further
Q1. Why might comparing a stock's P/E ratio to its industry average be more meaningful than looking at the P/E ratio in isolation?
A. Different industries typically trade at different normal P/E ranges, so a fair comparison needs a like-for-like industry benchmark
B. P/E ratios are identical across all industries by regulation
C. P/E ratio has no connection to how a stock is priced
D. Industry averages are set by the government and never change
Hint: Think about comparing similar businesses to each other.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Different industries have different typical valuation ranges, so a P/E ratio is best judged against peers in the same industry rather than in isolation.

Q2. Why does a Debt-to-Equity ratio above 1 generally suggest higher risk for a company during an economic downturn?
A. The company relies more heavily on borrowed money, which must be repaid regardless of how the business is performing
B. It means the company has no revenue at all
C. It automatically means the company is committing fraud
D. It has no bearing on financial risk
Hint: Think about fixed repayment obligations versus the company's own capital.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Debt must be repaid on fixed terms even if business conditions worsen, so heavier reliance on debt adds risk during a downturn.

Q3. Index funds are recommended as a fallback for investors who can't answer the quantitative checklist. What is the main structural reason index funds don't require this individual company analysis?
A. They passively track a broad market index, spreading exposure across many companies rather than requiring analysis of one specific stock
B. They are managed exclusively by government agencies
C. They guarantee a fixed, government-backed return
D. They only invest in a single company chosen by the fund manager
Hint: Think about "passive" versus "picking one stock."
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Index funds spread money across many companies in a benchmark automatically, which removes the need to individually evaluate any single stock's numbers.

Q4. What is the main reason a checklist framework recommends checking "time horizon" before investing in equity specifically?
A. Equity can be volatile short-term, so money needed soon shouldn't be exposed to a potential dip right before it's required
B. Time horizon only matters for real estate investments
C. Time horizon determines the exact tax rate on any investment
D. Time horizon has no relationship to which asset class is appropriate
Hint: Think about matching the goal's timing to the asset's volatility.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Money needed soon shouldn't sit in a volatile asset like equity, since a downturn right before the money is needed could force a loss-making sale.

Q5. Consistent year-over-year revenue growth is one of the checklist's quantitative checks. Why is "consistency" emphasised, rather than just one good year?
A. A single strong year could be a one-off event, while sustained growth suggests a more durable underlying business
B. Consistency has no bearing on investment quality
C. One strong year is always more reliable than multiple years of data
D. Revenue growth is unrelated to a company's long-term prospects
Hint: Think about the difference between a fluke and a trend.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Growth sustained over several years is more likely to reflect a genuinely strong, durable business rather than a one-time or temporary boost.
