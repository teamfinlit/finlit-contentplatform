## Quick Spark
Asset allocation means deciding how much money goes into equity, debt, gold, real estate, and cash — before you ever pick a single stock. A simple starting rule: keep (100 minus your age) percent in equity and the rest in debt. Get the mix right first; the mix matters more than any single pick.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: What Asset Allocation Really Means
Every rupee you invest has to live somewhere — in shares of a company, in a loan you make to a government or business, in gold, in property, or simply in cash. Asset allocation is the decision of how much goes into each of these buckets, made before choosing any specific investment. This first-level decision consistently explains far more of the difference between two investors' results than which particular stock or fund either of them picked inside their mix.
Example: Maanu, 17, has ₹10,000 saved. Instead of asking "which stock should I buy?", she first decides ₹7,000 goes to equity funds, ₹2,000 to a debt fund, and ₹1,000 to gold — then picks the specific fund inside each bucket.

LEARNING POINT 2: The Five Major Asset Classes
- Equity — ownership in companies through shares or mutual funds; grows fastest over long periods but swings the most in the short term.
- Debt — lending money to a government or company (bonds, fixed deposits, debt funds) for steady interest; safer, but grows slowly.
- Gold — a store of value that tends to hold up or gain when other assets fall, especially during uncertainty.
- Real Estate — land or property; can earn rent and appreciate, but is hard to sell quickly and needs large capital.
- Cash — money kept liquid in a savings account; earns almost nothing but is instantly usable.
Each class tends to behave differently in the same year, and that difference is exactly what makes mixing them worthwhile.

LEARNING POINT 3: The Age-Based Allocation Rule
The course rule: put (100 − your age) percent in equity and the rest in debt. A 20-year-old holds 80% equity / 20% debt; a 60-year-old holds 40% equity / 60% debt. Equity is volatile in the short run but tends to reward patience over long stretches, and a younger person has more years ahead to ride out a bad patch before actually needing the money. As a goal or retirement gets closer, the rule shifts money toward debt because there is less time left to recover from a downturn.

LEARNING POINT 4: Sample Portfolios Across Life Stages
- Aggressive (age 20–30): 80% equity, 10% gold, 10% debt
- Balanced (age 30–45): 60% equity, 15% gold, 25% debt
- Conservative (age 45+): 40% equity, 20% gold, 40% debt
These are starting templates, not rigid laws — they get adjusted for a person's goals, income stability, and comfort with risk.
Example: CA Jay, 47, broadly follows the Conservative template but keeps a little extra equity because his income is stable and his goals are still ten years away.

LEARNING POINT 5: International Exposure
Adding roughly 5–10% of a portfolio to global funds or foreign stocks means your money isn't riding on only one country's economy, currency, and market cycle. If the domestic market or currency has a rough year, international holdings can move differently and cushion the overall portfolio.

LEARNING POINT 6: Emergency Fund Sits Outside the Portfolio
Money set aside for a job loss, medical need, or urgent repair is not one of the allocation buckets — it is kept separate, in cash or a liquid fund, decided before any allocation choice is made. Folding it into the "portfolio" is a mistake because emergency money must be reachable instantly, without waiting on market timing.

## Real-Life Fact
According to Reserve Bank of India household savings data, a large share of Indian household wealth has historically sat in physical assets — gold and real estate — with a much smaller share held directly in equities and mutual funds. That imbalance is exactly why deliberate asset-allocation literacy matters: most households already know how to save, but far fewer have consciously chosen how to split that saving across asset classes.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Believing that finding "the one great stock" matters more than getting the overall mix right.
Correction: Even a brilliant single stock pick inside a portfolio with the wrong mix (say, 100% equity for someone one year from a goal) can still leave you exposed to a bad crash at the worst possible time. Decide the mix first; pick the specific investment second.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Decide how you divide your money before you decide what to buy — the mix does more work than the pick.

Using today's approximate values, work out what percentage sits in each asset class, and compare that mix to the age-rule template for your own age (or a family member's). Write down one adjustment you would suggest and why.

## Check Your Thinking
Q1. What does "asset allocation" mean in investing?
A. Dividing your money across different types of assets like equity, debt, and gold
B. Picking the single best stock in the market
C. Timing when to buy and sell shares
D. Keeping all your money in a savings account
Hint: Think about "dividing," not "picking."
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Asset allocation is about the mix across asset classes — equity, debt, gold, real estate, and cash — not about choosing one stock or timing the market.

Q2. Using the course's age rule, how much equity should a 25-year-old hold?
A. 25%
B. 50%
C. 75%
D. 100%
Hint: The rule is (100 − age)% in equity.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: 100 − 25 = 75, so 75% equity and 25% debt is the rule-of-thumb starting point.

Q3. Rohan is 40 years old and wants to follow the age rule strictly. How much of his portfolio should be in debt?
A. 40%
B. 60%
C. 25%
D. 75%
Hint: First find the equity share, then subtract from 100.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Equity share = 100 − 40 = 60%, so the remaining 40% goes to debt.

Q4. Why does the lesson say an emergency fund should NOT be counted as part of your investment portfolio?
A. Emergency money must stay instantly accessible and safe, not exposed to market ups and downs
B. Emergency funds are illegal to invest
C. Emergency funds always earn higher returns than equity
D. Emergency funds should be invested only in gold
Hint: Think about what an emergency actually requires — speed, or growth?
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: An emergency fund exists to be used immediately in a crisis, so it needs to sit in cash or a liquid fund, separate from money allocated for long-term growth.

Q5. A 52-year-old investor wants a portfolio close to the course's sample templates. Which allocation best matches the "Conservative" template for age 45+?
A. 80% equity, 10% gold, 10% debt
B. 60% equity, 15% gold, 25% debt
C. 40% equity, 20% gold, 40% debt
D. 100% equity, 0% gold, 0% debt
Hint: Match the age band to the right template.
Correct Answer: C
Concept / Fact Explanation: The Conservative template (age 45+) is 40% equity, 20% gold, 40% debt — built for someone with a shorter time horizon before needing the money.

## Explore Further
Q1. Adding 5–10% international exposure to a portfolio mainly protects against which specific risk?
A. The risk of one country's economy, currency, or market underperforming at the same time
B. The risk of inflation only
C. The risk of paying higher taxes
D. The risk of a company going bankrupt
Hint: Think about what changes when you invest outside your home country.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Domestic-only portfolios are fully exposed to one economy's cycles and currency moves; global exposure diversifies that specific country risk.

Q2. Which Indian data source is most authoritative for tracking how much of household wealth sits in physical assets like gold and real estate versus financial assets like equity?
A. Reserve Bank of India (RBI) household savings data
B. A private blog ranking stocks
C. A film magazine
D. A weather forecasting agency
Hint: Look for the country's central bank.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: The RBI regularly publishes household financial savings data, the authoritative source for how Indian households actually allocate wealth.

Q3. Why do sample portfolios typically reduce equity and increase debt as the target age band rises?
A. Because older investors generally have less time to recover from a market downturn before needing their money
B. Because equity becomes illegal to hold after age 45
C. Because debt instruments always pay higher returns than equity
D. Because gold prices always fall after age 45
Hint: Think about time horizon, not returns.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: A shorter time horizon means less room to wait out a downturn, so allocations shift toward more stable, lower-volatility assets like debt.

Q4. Real estate is included as a major asset class but is often considered less flexible than equity or debt mutual funds. What is the main reason?
A. It requires large capital and cannot usually be sold quickly (illiquidity)
B. It never generates any income
C. It is banned for individual investors in India
D. It always loses value over time
Hint: Think about how quickly you could turn a house into cash.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Real estate typically needs a large upfront investment and can take months to sell, making it far less liquid than shares or bonds.

Q5. Historically, one argument for holding some gold in a portfolio (alongside equity and debt) is that gold tends to:
A. Hold or gain value during periods when equity markets fall sharply
B. Move in exactly the same direction as equities at all times
C. Guarantee a fixed rate of return every year
D. Be more liquid than a savings account
Hint: Think about "uncorrelated" behavior.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Gold has historically often held up or risen when equity markets fell sharply, which is why it is used as a stabilising asset in a diversified mix — though this is a tendency, not a guarantee.
