# Rebalancing
**Level:** 7-8 Intermediate Investor | **Chapter:** 2 - Portfolio Construction | **Topic:** 3

## Key Concepts
- Keeping Your Portfolio Balanced
- Target allocation
- Overweight/underweight
- Sell high
- Buy low
- Annual review
- >5% drift

## Quick Spark
Rebalancing means bringing your portfolio back to its original target mix after market moves shift it. Because it forces you to trim what's grown (sell high) and top up what's lagged (buy low), it's one of the few truly automatic ways to enforce investing discipline.

## Understand It
LEARNING POINT 1: Why Your Allocation Drifts on Its Own
Even if you never touch your portfolio, its mix changes over time simply because different assets grow at different rates. If equity has a strong year while debt grows only modestly, equity's share of the total naturally rises above your original target — without you buying a single extra share.

LEARNING POINT 2: A Concrete Example of Drift
Someone who starts with a 70% equity / 30% debt portfolio may find that, after a strong equity bull run, their portfolio has quietly become 80% equity / 20% debt — meaning they now carry more risk than they originally intended, purely because of market movement, not choice.

LEARNING POINT 3: What Rebalancing Actually Means
Rebalancing is the act of selling some of the "overweight" asset (the one that has grown beyond its target share) and buying more of the "underweight" asset (the one that has fallen below its target share), to restore the original target percentages.

LEARNING POINT 4: Why Rebalancing Forces "Sell High, Buy Low"
This is the powerful part: trimming an overweight asset means selling it after it has risen (selling high); topping up an underweight asset means buying it after it has fallen behind (buying low). Most investors struggle to do this on their own because it feels emotionally wrong to sell something that's "winning" — rebalancing removes that emotional decision and makes the discipline automatic and rule-based, rather than dependent on gut feeling.
Example: Dia, 42, follows a 60/25/15 equity-debt-gold target. After a strong equity year pushes her mix to 70/20/10, she sells a slice of equity and adds it to debt and gold, restoring her original target — automatically "selling high."

LEARNING POINT 5: When to Rebalance
Two common triggers: a calendar-based annual review (check once a year, regardless of what happened), or a drift-based trigger (rebalance whenever any asset class moves more than 5 percentage points away from its target). Either approach turns rebalancing into a habit rather than a reaction to market headlines.

## Real-Life Fact
A number of large Indian mutual fund houses build systematic, rule-based rebalancing into "balanced advantage" and dynamic asset allocation fund structures precisely because it is difficult for individual investors to sell their own winners and buy their own laggards — automated rebalancing rules remove that emotional friction by making the decision run on a preset formula rather than a feeling.

## Common Mistake
Common Mistake: Believing that letting your winning asset "ride" forever is the smart move because it's performing well.
Correction: An asset that keeps growing as a share of your portfolio is quietly increasing your risk exposure beyond what you originally decided was comfortable — rebalancing isn't about doubting a winner, it's about controlling risk.

## Key Takeaway
Key Takeaway: Rebalancing is discipline you don't have to feel — it automatically sells high and buys low for you.

## Try It in Real Life
If you (or a family member) hold more than one type of saving or investment, note down today's approximate split between them. Six months to a year from now, check the split again and calculate how far it has drifted from where it started. Decide whether it has moved more than 5 percentage points, and what a rebalancing move would look like.

## Check Your Thinking

B. Selling everything and moving entirely to cash
C. Picking a new stock every month
D. Ignoring your portfolio completely
Hint: Think "back to target."
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Rebalancing restores a portfolio's asset mix to the percentages originally targeted.

Q2. A portfolio started at 70% equity / 30% debt and, after a strong equity run, is now 80% equity / 20% debt. What has happened?
A. The portfolio has drifted and now carries more equity risk than originally intended
B. The portfolio automatically rebalanced itself
C. The debt allocation increased on its own
D. Nothing has changed in the risk profile
Hint: Which asset class grew as a share of the total?
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Equity's faster growth increased its share of the portfolio, raising the overall risk level beyond the original target.

Q3. To rebalance the 80/20 portfolio above back to 70/30, what should the investor do?
A. Sell some equity and buy more debt
B. Sell all the debt and buy only equity
C. Buy more equity and sell all the debt
D. Do nothing, since 80/20 is always better
Hint: Trim the overweight asset, top up the underweight one.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: Selling a slice of the overweight equity and adding it to the underweight debt restores the original 70/30 target.

Q4. Why is rebalancing described as forcing you to "sell high, buy low" automatically?
A. Because trimming the grown (overweight) asset means selling after it rose, and topping up the lagging (underweight) asset means buying after it fell behind
B. Because rebalancing guarantees you will only buy at the exact market bottom
C. Because rebalancing eliminates all investment risk completely
D. Because rebalancing only applies to real estate
Hint: Think about which asset rose and which one you top up.
Correct Answer: A
Concept / Fact Explanation: The mechanics of restoring targets naturally mean selling the asset that has risen and buying the one that has lagged.

Q5. Which of these is a valid trigger for rebalancing, according to the lesson?

## Explore Further
Q1. Why is rebalancing considered a "discipline mechanism" rather than a market-timing strategy?
A. It follows a fixed rule (time- or drift-based) rather than trying to predict future price movements
B. It requires guessing the exact top and bottom of the market
C. It relies entirely on tips from financial news anchors
D. It only works if you have insider information
Hint: Rule-based versus prediction-based.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Rebalancing follows a predetermined rule, so it doesn't depend on correctly predicting market direction.

Q2. "Balanced advantage" or dynamic asset allocation mutual funds often build in systematic rebalancing. What investor problem does this address?
A. The emotional difficulty of selling assets that are currently performing well
B. The legal requirement to hold only one asset class
C. The need to avoid ever paying taxes
D. The requirement to invest only in government bonds
Hint: Think about why individual investors struggle to do this themselves.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Automated rules remove the emotional resistance most investors feel toward selling a currently winning asset.

Q3. If an investor never rebalances over many years of a strong equity bull market, what is the most likely long-term consequence?
A. Their portfolio becomes progressively more equity-heavy and riskier than originally intended
B. Their portfolio automatically becomes more conservative
C. Their debt allocation will keep increasing on its own
D. Nothing changes because allocations are fixed once chosen
Hint: Think about compounding drift over multiple bull-market years.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Without rebalancing, a rising equity share compounds year after year, steadily pushing the portfolio further from its original risk level.

Q4. Compare a 5%-drift-triggered rebalancing rule to a strict once-a-year calendar rule. What's a key practical difference?
A. The drift rule can trigger rebalancing at any time the allocation moves too far, while the calendar rule only checks on a fixed date regardless of drift
B. The two rules are functionally identical in every situation
C. The calendar rule can never be combined with a drift rule
D. The drift rule requires selling the entire portfolio every time
Hint: One is time-based, one is threshold-based.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: A drift rule responds to how far the portfolio has actually moved, while a calendar rule responds only to the passage of time.

Q5. What is the main risk of rebalancing too frequently (e.g., every single week) in a normal market?
A. Higher transaction costs and possible taxes on gains, without meaningfully improving the outcome
B. It eliminates all investment returns immediately
C. It is illegal under SEBI regulations
D. It guarantees a loss every time
Hint: Think about costs versus benefit of over-frequent trading.
Correct Answer: A
Fact / Concept Explanation: Rebalancing too often adds unnecessary transaction costs and tax events without adding meaningful benefit over an annual or drift-based schedule.
